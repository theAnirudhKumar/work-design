# Methods cheat-sheet for sort-my-tasks

Short notes on the prioritisation methods this skill applies, so it works when installed alone. Evidence labels follow one rubric: Strong (repeated controlled studies support the core mechanism), Some (small, indirect or related-setting support), Mixed (studies conflict or depend on the person), Practice only (widely used, little direct testing). When unsure, the label is Practice only. No figures are claimed.

## Choosing quickly

| The list is | Use |
| :--- | :--- |
| Mixed daily or weekly items | Eisenhower |
| Tomorrow's cut, strict order wanted | Ivy Lee |
| A day's shape with mixed sizes | 1-3-5 |
| Projects or options with measurable value | RICE (or impact/effort if values cannot be estimated) |
| Scope against a deadline | MoSCoW |
| Commitments and invitations | Hell yeah or no |

## Eisenhower matrix
Sort items by urgent and important: do now (both), schedule (important, not urgent), delegate (urgent, not important), delete (neither).
- **How it is used here**: split the list into the four boxes, then rank inside the boxes. Most of the gain is naming the urgent-but-unimportant box.
- **Backfires when**: everything is judged urgent and important, or "important" is never defined. Fix: define important as "moves one of my outcomes" and cap the top box. It also does not compare two important things with each other.
- **Evidence**: Practice only. Often attributed to Dwight Eisenhower; the attribution is loosely sourced. The four-box form was popularised by a later self-help author.

## Ivy Lee method
Write the six most important tasks, rank them, work them in order without skipping, carry unfinished items to the next list.
- **How it is used here**: cut a list to six and number them. Anything below six goes to Delete, Defer, Delegate or Not doing.
- **Backfires when**: the day is reactive, so an ordered list cannot hold, or six is the wrong number for the person's capacity. Nothing in the method protects against ranking by comfort.
- **Evidence**: Practice only. A popular story attributes it to a consultant named Ivy Lee; the details are anecdotal.

## 1-3-5
One big, three medium, five small items.
- **How it is used here**: sort items into sizes and cap each size. If nine do not fit, use 1-2-3.
- **Backfires when**: the work has no natural small tasks, or the caps are filled with the easy items while the big one is skipped.
- **Evidence**: Practice only. Folk practice, no single origin.

## RICE
Score each option on reach, impact, confidence and effort; priority is (reach times impact times confidence) divided by effort.
- **How it is used here**: for competing projects or features where some numbers can be estimated. Estimates are labelled as guesses.
- **Backfires when**: the numbers are invented to justify a favourite, or the scores are treated as precise. Low-confidence items should drop, which is what the confidence term is for. A poor fit for a personal to-do list.
- **Evidence**: Practice only. Popularised by a software company's product team; it is a scoring convention, not a tested model.

## Impact/effort
Place options on a grid of expected impact against effort. Do high impact, low effort first; question low impact, high effort.
- **How it is used here**: when numbers are not available and a rough high/low call is enough.
- **Backfires when**: "impact" is vague, effort is underestimated (as it commonly is), or quick wins crowd out the one hard thing that matters.
- **Evidence**: Practice only.

## MoSCoW
Label each item must, should, could or won't (this time).
- **How it is used here**: for scope under a deadline. The musts must fit the time on their own; if they do not, the problem is the deadline, not the list, and the hand-off is `fit-to-deadline`.
- **Backfires when**: everything is a must. Cap the musts at what fits, and write the won't list as a real list. "Won't" means not this time, not never.
- **Evidence**: Practice only. Comes from agile software delivery.

## Hell yeah or no
For a commitment or invitation, say yes only when your reaction is strong enthusiasm; otherwise say no.
- **How it is used here**: for requests, invitations and new commitments in the list. It is a filter for yeses, not a ranking.
- **Backfires when**: obligations that are not optional (care, deadlines, jobs) are treated as if they were choices, or the person has little say over their time. Then use Eisenhower for the obligations and keep this for what is optional.
- **Evidence**: Practice only. Popularised by a writer and entrepreneur in a short book of the same name.

## The four-way sort: Delete, Defer, Delegate, Not doing
Every item outside the top group goes somewhere on purpose.
- **Delete**: finished, obsolete, or not yours.
- **Defer**: later, with a date or trigger.
- **Delegate**: to a person with a one-line ask.
- **Not doing**: valid, deliberately not done this horizon, with the cost accepted in one line.
- **Backfires when**: Defer is used for everything and has no dates, which turns it into a second backlog. An undated defer is a delete or a not-doing.
- **Evidence**: Practice only. The idea of deciding and not just postponing is common to many task systems.

## First physical action
The next thing the hands or eyes do, small and visible, so the next step is obvious.
- **How it is used here**: for the top 3 only.
- **Backfires when**: it is really a plan ("figure out the budget"), which delays the start. If the action cannot be named, the item may be stuck for a reason, and `get-unstuck` fits.
- **Evidence**: Practice only. A starting move that is small and specific is common coaching advice.
