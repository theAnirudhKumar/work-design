# Get Unstuck

Advice for a stalled task is usually the same advice whatever the cause: break it down, just start, block out time. That fits one cause in nine and makes the other eight worse. Someone waiting on a reply does not need a smaller task. Someone who should not be doing the work at all does not need willpower.

The failure this exists to prevent: **applying a generic push to a task that is stalled for a specific reason, so the person tries harder at the wrong thing and concludes the problem is them.**

Diagnose first, then give one move that fits the cause.

---

## What this needs

**Minimum: a sentence on what the work is and that it is stalled.** The skill diagnoses from whatever the user said and asks at most two questions.

**Better with** how long it has been stuck, what was tried, and who or what else is involved. These separate "waiting on someone" from "avoiding it".

**Best with** the deadline and what happens if it slips, which decides whether the answer can be "drop it".

Nothing is a prerequisite. No files, calendar or task tool. If a task or calendar tool is connected, the first action can be put on it as an optional upgrade, but only on request.

---

## When this runs

Runs when one specific piece of work has stalled and the user wants it moving, or wants to know why it is not.

**Does not run for:**

- Work that has not started and needs its edges drawn. `scope-the-work` handles that, and the `plan-the-work` skill frames a vague start.
- Work that is too large for the time available. The `fit-to-deadline` skill fits scope to a budget. Here, "too big to start" is one possible cause, and the move is a first slice, not a re-scope.
- A plan that might fail for reasons not yet found. The `what-could-go-wrong` skill handles that.
- Deciding who should do the work. `delegate` handles that; this skill hands off to it where the cause points there.
- Distress, burnout or a pattern across all of the user's work. See the boundary in Step 6.

---

## Step 1: Read what was given before asking anything

Look for tells in the user's own words. Match them against `references/causes.md`, which lists nine causes with the tell and the move for each. Quote the phrase that points to the cause; the diagnosis must rest on something the user said, not on a guess about them.

If the message already points clearly to one cause, skip to Step 3.

## Step 2: Ask at most two questions

Only when two or more causes remain plausible. Prefer one pick-from-list question over open ones:

> Which of these sounds most like it? (a) I am not sure what "done" looks like, (b) it is huge and I cannot see a first step, (c) I am waiting on someone or something, (d) there is a decision I have not made, (e) I am worried how it will land, (f) it is boring or unpleasant, (g) other things matter more, (h) I am wrong-footed on time or energy for it.

A second question, if needed, is "what happens if this does not get done?", which separates "competing priority" and "not needed" from the rest. Never ask a third. If the answers are still ambiguous, name the top two causes and give the move for the likelier one.

## Step 3: State the diagnosis in one sentence

Format: the cause, then the evidence. "This looks like an undecided decision hiding inside the task: you said you cannot write the section until you know which vendor you are going with." One cause. Where two are mixed, lead with the one that blocks the other and mention the second after the action.

State it as a read, not a verdict: "looks like", and the user is free to say it does not fit. If they say so, re-diagnose once from what they add.

## Step 4: Give ONE next action of 15 minutes or less

Concrete enough to start without further thought: **verb + object + stopping point.** "Write the three questions the vendor needs answered, in a note, then stop" passes. "Think about the vendor decision" does not. The stopping point matters: it is what makes starting less costly.

Always give the fallback: **"If that is still too big, do this 5-minute version:"** and a smaller version that keeps the same direction, not a different task.

Match the move to the cause using `references/causes.md`. Do not default to "break it into steps".

## Step 5: Schedule the first move with an if-then

Write one implementation intention (Gollwitzer 1999): **"If [specific cue the user will actually meet], then I [the action]."** The cue must be something that happens anyway: "If I sit down with my first coffee tomorrow", "If the 2pm call ends", "If I open my laptop after lunch". Not "if I have time". Ask the user to adjust the cue to their real day; do not claim it works for everyone, only that it moves the decision of when out of the moment.

## Step 6: Handle the external and the optional causes

**If the cause is external** (waiting on an input or a person, or a decision that belongs to someone else): do not give a self-help action. Name the exact thing to send or force, and draft it.

- The message: who it goes to, the one question or request, the date needed by, and what happens if there is no reply. Keep it short enough to send as written. If the right channel is unclear, hand off to `meeting-or-message`.
- The decision: state it as a question with two or three options and a deadline, and name who has the right to decide. If the user is the one who must decide, the action is to write the options and the criteria, then choose.
- If the real fix is giving the part to someone else, hand off to `delegate`.
- Also give one action for what the user can do in the meantime, if any exists. If none does, say so; waiting is not a failure.

**If the work looks unnecessary or lower priority** (competing priority, not needed): offer to drop or defer it, as an explicit option with a stated consequence. "Drop it: nobody has asked about it in three weeks, and the cost is that the quarterly summary will have a gap you can mention in one line." "Defer to the 14th: put the date on it and remove it from this week's list." Say which the evidence favours. Never present continuing as the only option.

**Boundary.** This is a work-process skill. Do not diagnose anxiety, ADHD, depression or any condition, and do not give medical advice, even if the user offers a label. If the user describes distress beyond work friction (not sleeping, dread about everything, feeling hopeless, being unable to function), say once, kindly and without alarm, that a person who can help is worth talking to, and that this is more than a task problem. Then ask whether they want the practical part anyway, and continue only if they do. Do not repeat the suggestion.

## Output

In this order, each short:

1. **Diagnosis**, one sentence with the evidence quoted from the user.
2. **Next action**, verb + object + stopping point, 15 minutes or less.
3. **5-minute fallback.**
4. **If-then**, with a cue from the user's real day.
5. **If the cause is external:** the drafted message or the forced decision, and the in-the-meantime action. **If the work may be unnecessary:** the drop or defer option with its consequence.
6. **What I had to assume or could not check**, including any cause I could not rule out.

For a reusable version the user can run alone next time, point them to `assets/stuck-check.md`.

---

## Failure modes

**Giving the same advice for every cause.** If the action reads as "break it into smaller steps" regardless of diagnosis, Step 1 was skipped.

**Diagnosing from the topic instead of the words.** "Taxes" does not mean avoidance. Use only what the user said or answered.

**An action that is really a plan.** "Make a plan for the report" is 15 minutes of planning that feels like progress and leaves the stall untouched. The action must produce a piece of the work or a sent message, except where the cause is an unclear outcome, in which case writing the done-sentence is the work.

**A third question.** Past two, the user is being interviewed while stuck. Diagnose from what exists.

**Motivational language.** No "you've got this", no "just do it". Warm and brisk: say what it looks like and what to do.

**Ignoring the drop option.** If the evidence says the work does not matter, say so. Helping someone push through work that should be dropped is a failure of this skill.

**Playing therapist.** Naming a condition, or asking about feelings beyond what the work needs. Stay on the work.

**A cue that never happens.** An if-then tied to a day that has no such moment ("if I have a free hour") gets skipped. Ask for one that already occurs.
