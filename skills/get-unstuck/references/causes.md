# The nine causes of a stall

For each: the tell (what the user's words tend to sound like), the move (the 15-minute action that fits), and what not to do. A tell is a lead, not proof. Ask when two fit.

## 1. The outcome is unclear

**Tell:** "I am not sure what they want", "I do not know what good looks like", "I keep rewriting the first paragraph".
**Move:** Write the done-sentence in one line, then send it to whoever asked with "Is this what you need?" If nobody asked, write what the finished thing would let someone do that they cannot do now.
**Not:** Start producing. Work without a target drifts.

## 2. Too big to start

**Tell:** "It is huge", "I do not know where to begin", a task title that is a project ("Sort out the website").
**Move:** Name the smallest piece that produces something visible or checkable, and do it to a stopping point: the first section, one screen, one customer's records, the first ten rows. A slice of the real work, not a plan of it.
**Not:** A full breakdown. Listing 40 steps is its own stall.

## 3. Waiting on an input or a person

**Tell:** "I need X from them first", "I sent it and heard nothing", "I cannot do the next bit until..."
**Move:** Send the chaser. Name the exact message: one request, a needed-by date, and what happens without it. Then do anything that does not depend on the input.
**Not:** More effort on the part you can see. The stall is outside you.

## 4. An undecided decision is hiding inside the task

**Tell:** "I cannot write it until I know whether...", "I keep going back and forth on...", the work restarts at the same point each time.
**Move:** Pull the decision out. Write it as a question with two or three options and the one or two criteria that would settle it. Decide, or name who must and by when. Then the task continues.
**Not:** Working on the task as if the decision were made.

## 5. Perfectionism or fear of the result being judged

**Tell:** "It is not good enough yet", "I do not want to send it until it is right", "what if they hate it", a draft that exists but never goes out.
**Move:** Produce a deliberately rough version in 15 minutes, labelled as rough, with a stated stopping point, for one named reader who will react to it. Or set the question for review: "what is the one thing you would change?" Lower the stakes of the first version; do not try to remove the fear.
**Not:** Reassurance, or a diagnosis. Keep it about the draft.

## 6. The task is dull or unpleasant

**Tell:** "I just hate doing these", "it is so tedious", the task is clerical and clear but untouched.
**Move:** Do the first ten minutes bound to a timer, paired with something that makes it bearable (a call you were going to make, a place you like). Or ask whether it can be shrunk, batched with the next occurrence, or handed off, and if so go to `delegate`.
**Not:** Treating dullness as a character flaw. It is a normal reason for delay and it is fixed by changing the conditions.

## 7. Competing priorities: it is not the most important thing

**Tell:** "I will get to it when things calm down", "there is always something more urgent", it has moved from list to list.
**Move:** Ask what happens if it is not done. If little, offer defer-with-a-date or drop, and state the consequence. If real, protect a time block for it and say what moves out to make room.
**Not:** More determination. It may be the right call to leave it.

## 8. Wrong energy or time of day

**Tell:** "I can never do this after lunch", "I keep trying at the end of the day when I am drained", it needs focus and gets the dregs.
**Move:** Move the work to the part of the day where the user has the energy for this kind of work. Put the first 15 minutes there, with an if-then on a real cue. If the work is low-focus, do the opposite: use the low-energy slot for it.
**Not:** Pushing through at the same hour again.

## 9. The work is genuinely unnecessary

**Tell:** "Nobody has asked for it", "I am not sure anyone will use it", "it was someone's idea months ago", no named reader or consequence.
**Move:** Check with the one person who would know: "Is this still needed? If I drop it, what is lost?" If the answer is nothing, drop it and tell whoever else expected it. Delete it from the list so it stops costing attention.
**Not:** Completing it to clear the guilt.

## When causes combine

Pick the one that blocks the others and move on that. Typical pairs: a hidden decision (4) with fear of judgement (5), where the decision is what is being avoided; too big (2) with an unclear outcome (1), where the outcome comes first; competing priorities (7) with unnecessary (9), where checking need settles both.
