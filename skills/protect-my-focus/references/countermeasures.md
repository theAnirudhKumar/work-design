# Countermeasures and messages

Evidence labels: Strong, Some, Mixed, Practice only. Be honest: most of these are widely used and sensible, but little is tested in the form the user will use it. Judge them by the two-week experiment.

## The countermeasures

### 1. Notification batching
- **What:** Turn off per-message alerts for non-urgent apps; check them at set times (for example on the hour, or three times a day).
- **Best for:** phone and email banners, social, news, shopping.
- **Backfires when:** the role requires fast response, or one important sender is hiding in the noise. Fix: keep a short allow-list (named people, the one critical channel) and batch the rest.
- **Evidence:** Some. A few studies of limiting or batching notifications and email checks suggest less stress and focus benefits for some people, with mixed effects on performance.

### 2. Do Not Disturb windows
- **What:** A scheduled daily window (for example 9 to 11) when the phone and desktop silence everything except named exceptions.
- **Best for:** protected deep work in a role that allows it; a morning or evening block.
- **Backfires when:** people are not told, so the silence reads as being ignored; or no exceptions are set for emergencies. Pair with a status signal.
- **Evidence:** Practice only.

### 3. Email and chat batching windows
- **What:** Open email and chat at fixed times (for example 10:30, 13:30, 16:30) and close them in between.
- **Best for:** knowledge work with reply times measured in hours.
- **Backfires when:** the team's culture expects instant replies, or the user is the escalation point. Needs a response-time norm first.
- **Evidence:** Some for email batching; chat batching is Practice only.

### 4. A maker's schedule
- **What:** Keep half-day or longer blocks for making (building, writing, analysing) and move the manager-style work (meetings, calls, ad hoc help) into the other part of the day or week. The idea is described in a well-known essay by Paul Graham on makers and managers.
- **Best for:** engineers, writers, designers, researchers.
- **Backfires when:** the person's manager or customers work on a manager's schedule and will not adapt; then negotiate hours, not a whole day.
- **Evidence:** Practice only.

### 5. Meeting-free blocks
- **What:** A fixed day or half-day (for the user or the team) with no meetings. Examples: no meetings Wednesday, or mornings.
- **Best for:** teams where meetings fragment the day.
- **Backfires when:** it is declared and not defended, so meetings creep back in; or the meetings simply move to the neighbouring days and pile up.
- **Evidence:** Practice only; some organisations report it works, with little controlled testing. For cleaning up the meetings themselves, hand off to `meeting-audit`.

### 6. Status signals
- **What:** A visible cue that says "interrupt only if it is urgent": headphones on, a sign, a chat status with a return time, a closed door.
- **Best for:** open offices, shared homes, and chat-heavy teams.
- **Backfires when:** nobody agreed what the signal means, or it is on all day and trusted by no one. Agree a meaning and an exception.
- **Evidence:** Practice only.

### 7. A team response-time norm
- **What:** An explicit team agreement on how fast each channel is answered: for example urgent by phone, chat within a few hours, email within a day.
- **Best for:** the root cause of "everything feels urgent"; it removes the fear that makes people check constantly.
- **Backfires when:** it is a decree from one person with no buy-in, or the true urgent route is not defined.
- **Evidence:** Practice only. Underlies most of the others.

### 8. Working from a different location
- **What:** Move the focus block to a place the usual interrupters are not: a meeting room, a library, home for the morning, a quiet floor.
- **Best for:** drop-ins and open offices.
- **Backfires when:** the move itself eats time, or it makes the user unreachable for something that matters; or it cannot be explained and looks like hiding. Say where you are and when you will reply.
- **Evidence:** Practice only.

### 9. Designing for interruptible work
- **What:** For roles where interruption is the job: keep a resume note (write the exact stopping point before switching), use queues and rotas so only one person is first-line at a time, build short protected windows (15 to 30 minutes) with a named cover, and group non-urgent requests into a daily huddle.
- **Best for:** support, nursing, reception, teaching, caregiving, on-call.
- **Backfires when:** no cover exists, so the protected window is a fiction; or the queue becomes another source of pings.
- **Evidence:** Practice only.

## Message templates

*Fit the numbers and names to the user's job. The people and teams are invented.*

### To a manager: protected hours
> Hi Priya, I want to try something for two weeks and I'd like your OK. I'm planning to keep 9:00 to 11:00 on Tuesday to Thursday for the pricing model, with chat closed and my phone on Do Not Disturb. In return, I'll check messages at 11:00 and 15:00 every day, and if something truly can't wait, call me and I'll pick up. I'd like to measure how many of those blocks I actually get through, and we can review on [date] and keep it, change it or drop it. Does that work?

Fallback ask: one block, one day a week.

### To a manager: a response-time norm
> Hi Sam, a lot of my day goes to checking whether something urgent has landed in chat. Could we agree a rule for the team: urgent means a phone call, chat gets an answer within three hours, email within a day? That would let me close chat for blocks without worrying I'm missing something. I'm happy to draft it and bring it to the next team meeting. We can try it for two weeks.

### To a team: chat batching
> Heads up from me, trial for two weeks: I'll be in focus blocks 9:00 to 11:00 and 14:00 to 15:30, and I'll answer chat at 11:00, 13:00 and 15:30. If it's urgent, ring me. Everything else gets a reply at the next check. I'll tell you if it's not working for you, and I'd like to hear it if it isn't.

### To the one main interrupter
> Hi Tomas, I'd like to get you quick answers and I also need long stretches on the audit. Could we set a 15-minute check-in at 10:00 each day for your questions, and save the rest for then unless it's blocking you? If something is on fire, call. Does a trial for two weeks work?

### Interruptible role: asking for a cover window
> Hi Mei, on the ward I get asked things constantly, which is the job. But I need 20 uninterrupted minutes twice a shift for charting. Could we agree that one of us is first-line for those windows, and swap? I'll cover yours the same way. We can note how it goes for two weeks.

### Open office: a status signal
> Team, trial: when I've got my headphones on, I'm in a focus block for up to 90 minutes. Tap my shoulder or message me if it's urgent, and otherwise I'll come find you straight after. Happy to hear what doesn't work.

### Working elsewhere
> Hi Dana, I'm going to work from the quiet room on the third floor from 9:00 to 11:30 on Tuesdays and Thursdays to finish the report. I'll check chat at 11:30 and I'm reachable by phone if something urgent comes up. Two-week trial, then I'll tell you how it went.
