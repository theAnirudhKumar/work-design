# Evidence: estimate-from-precedent

This skill's core move, correcting a fresh estimate against how similar past work actually took rather than trusting the intuitive forecast for this instance, is built on two related, well-established findings.

**The planning fallacy.** Buehler, R., Griffin, D., & Ross, M., "Exploring the 'Planning Fallacy': Why People Underestimate Their Task Completion Times," *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 1994. The finding: people underestimate how long a task will take even when they have direct personal experience of similar tasks running long, because the estimate is built from an idealized scenario for this specific instance (the "inside view") rather than from the outcome of comparable past instances (the "outside view"). Knowing about the bias does not, by itself, correct it: the bias persists even among people who have been warned about it, which is why this skill asks for actual past durations as a mechanical step rather than just asking someone to "estimate more carefully."

**Reference class forecasting.** The corrective built from the same inside-view/outside-view distinction, most associated with later work by Flyvbjerg (for example "From Nobel Prize to Project Management: Getting Risks Right," *Project Management Journal*, 2006) applying it to large infrastructure and IT project forecasting: build the estimate from the distribution of outcomes in a class of genuinely comparable past cases, rather than from reasoning about the specific case in isolation. This skill's reference-class step (Step 2 in SKILL.md) is a direct, much smaller-scale application of that method to a single piece of work rather than a portfolio of projects.

This is cited as established, decades-old psychological and forecasting research, not a fresh dataset, and no search was run for the current state of that literature beyond confirming these are real, findable citations. Both papers are widely cited outside this repository and are not unique or unusual claims. No claim in this skill rests on a specific statistic from either paper; the citation is for the mechanism (inside view underestimates, outside view corrects it), not for a number to quote.
