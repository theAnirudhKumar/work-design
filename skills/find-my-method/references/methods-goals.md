# Methods: goals and planning

These methods turn a vague wish into something you can act on this week. They differ in how far ahead they look (a day to a year), how much structure they add, and how likely they are to turn into admin. Goal-setting research mostly supports the plain idea that specific, challenging goals with feedback beat vague ones. It supports far less the elaborate frameworks built on top of that idea. Pick the lightest method that gives you a clear next action, and drop any method that has you updating a tracker more than doing the work.

## OKRs
**One line:** Write one qualitative objective, then two to four measurable key results that show whether you got there, and review them on a fixed cycle.
**Best for:** Teams or solo people with several competing priorities who need to say what matters most this quarter and how they will know.
**Skip it when:** The work is reactive or unpredictable, you would only be inventing numbers, or the key results turn into a scoreboard that people game or chase at the cost of the real aim. Also skip it for a goal you can finish in a week.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Write one objective in plain words: a direction, not a number ("Make onboarding painless for new clients").
2. Under it, write two to four key results that someone else could check as met or not met.
3. Cross out any key result that measures activity ("send 50 emails") rather than a result you actually want.
4. Put a 10-minute check-in on your calendar once a week to rate each key result and name the next move.
**7-day trial:** Write one objective and two key results on Day 1. Each day, note the one action that moved a key result. On Day 7, rate each key result and ask whether the numbers changed what you did. Track: actions tied to a key result, and any day you chased the number instead of the aim.
**Pairs well with:** Weekly review; Milestone mapping; Goal ladder.
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin.
**Evidence:** Practice only. OKRs are a management practice widely used in companies with little rigorous testing of the framework itself, though the general idea of specific goals with feedback is better supported.
**Effort to set up:** Medium.   **Works for:** Either.

## SMART goals
**One line:** Rewrite a goal so it is specific, measurable, achievable, relevant and has a deadline.
**Best for:** A fuzzy wish ("get fit", "save more", "finish the thesis") that never turns into a first step.
**Skip it when:** The goal is exploratory or the outcome is outside your control, where a rigid target makes you stop learning or feel like a failure when conditions change. It also backfires if you keep refining wording and never act.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Write the goal as you would say it to a friend.
2. Add the specifics: what exactly, how you will count it, and by which date.
3. Check it against your real calendar. If the date or amount is not realistic, shrink one of them.
4. Write the first action and when you will do it.
**7-day trial:** Rewrite one goal in SMART form on Day 1. Do the first action by Day 2. Each day, log whether you did the planned step. On Day 7, check whether the target still feels right or needs adjusting. Track: days you did the step, and whether the target needed to change.
**Pairs well with:** WOOP; Milestone mapping; Weekly review.
**Comes from:** Often attributed to a 1981 management article by George Doran; the attribution is disputed in its details and the acronym has many variants.
**Evidence:** Some. Research on goal setting (Locke and Latham) supports specific, challenging goals with feedback over vague "do your best" goals, but that does not show the SMART checklist itself is what helps.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Either.

## WOOP
**One line:** Name a wish, picture the best outcome, name the main inner obstacle, then write an if-then plan for meeting it.
**Best for:** A goal you want but keep dropping at the same sticking point, such as tiredness, temptation or avoidance.
**Skip it when:** You are not yet committed to the goal (the exercise may show it is not worth pursuing, which is a fine result), or you would use it to spiral on worries. It is a short planning exercise, not therapy.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Wish: write one goal you can work toward in the next few weeks.
2. Outcome: write the best result and how it would feel, in a sentence or two.
3. Obstacle: write the main thing inside you that is likely to get in the way (a habit, a feeling, an excuse).
4. Plan: write "If [obstacle shows up], then I will [one specific action]." Keep it small enough to do on a bad day.
**7-day trial:** Do the four steps on Day 1 and put the if-then somewhere you will see it. Each day, note whether the obstacle appeared and whether you used the plan. Track: times the obstacle appeared, and times you ran the plan. Adjust the plan on Day 4 if it never fires.
**Pairs well with:** SMART goals; Anti-goals; Milestone mapping.
**Comes from:** Gabriele Oettingen's research on mental contrasting and implementation intentions.
**Evidence:** Some. Studies of mental contrasting with implementation intentions report benefits in several settings, though samples and settings vary, and positive visualising alone has mixed support.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Solo.

## Working backwards
**One line:** Start from the finished result, then step back through what had to be true just before it, until you reach what to do today.
**Best for:** A big project with a clear end state but no visible path, such as a launch, a thesis, a house move or a certification.
**Skip it when:** The end state is unknown and you need to explore first, or the route depends on things you cannot predict. A backward plan built on guesses gives false confidence.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Write the finished state in one or two sentences, as if it already happened, including the date.
2. Ask "what has to be done right before this?" and write the answer. Repeat until you have five to eight steps.
3. Mark any step that depends on another person or a decision, and add a date by which you need it.
4. Circle the earliest step and write the first 30-minute action.
**7-day trial:** Build the backward chain on Day 1. Each day, do one action on the earliest open step and tick it off. On Day 7, re-check the chain for steps you missed or ordered wrongly. Track: steps completed, and steps you had to add.
**Pairs well with:** Milestone mapping; Goal ladder; Weekly review.
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin.
**Evidence:** Practice only. It is used in project and product planning, but there is little direct testing of this method.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Either.

## Annual theme
**One line:** Choose one word or short phrase to steer the year, in place of a list of resolutions.
**Best for:** People who set many goals in January, drop them by March, and want a lighter way to guide choices.
**Skip it when:** You need measurable commitments (a licence exam, a debt target), or the word is so vague that it never changes a decision.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. List three things you want more of and three you want less of this year.
2. Find a word or phrase that covers the more-of list ("Steady", "Finish things", "Local").
3. Write three decisions it would change in the next month.
4. Put it where you will see it: phone lock screen, the top of your planner, a sticky note.
**7-day trial:** Pick a theme for the next quarter on Day 1. Each day, note one choice the theme influenced, however small. On Day 7, ask whether it changed anything real. If it never does, pick a more concrete one. Track: choices shaped by the theme, and days you forgot it.
**Pairs well with:** Annual review; Goal ladder; Anti-goals.
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin.
**Evidence:** Practice only. It is a popular habit with no direct testing, though it overlaps with the broader idea that fewer, clearer aims are easier to follow.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Solo.

## 12 Week Year
**One line:** Treat 12 weeks as the whole year: set a few goals, plan them week by week, and score your execution every week.
**Best for:** Someone with a clear set of goals who loses momentum over a long year and wants shorter, more urgent cycles.
**Skip it when:** Your work is seasonal, reactive or shaped by fixed annual events, or you would spend more time scoring yourself than working. A weekly score can turn into guilt about missed tasks when a goal needed to change instead.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Choose one to three goals for the next 12 weeks. More than three spreads you too thin.
2. For each goal, write the three to five actions that most drive it.
3. Spread those actions across the 12 weeks in a simple grid, with a lighter last week for review.
4. Set a weekly 15-minute slot to mark which planned actions you did.
**7-day trial:** Run one mini-week. On Day 1, plan the week's actions for one goal. Each day, tick what you did. On Day 7, compute done over planned and ask whether the actions were the right ones. Track: actions planned vs done, and one action that proved pointless.
**Pairs well with:** Weekly review; Milestone mapping; OKRs.
**Comes from:** Brian Moran and Michael Lennington popularised the name in their book of the same name.
**Evidence:** Practice only. It is a commercial planning system built on plausible ideas (short horizons, weekly review), but I know of no controlled tests of the method itself.
**Effort to set up:** Medium.   **Works for:** Either.

## Wheel of Life
**One line:** Rate your satisfaction in eight or so life areas on a circle, then look at which areas are lopsided.
**Best for:** A vague sense that something is off, or a start-of-year check on whether work has crowded out everything else.
**Skip it when:** You are in acute crisis or distress (see a professional instead), or you would treat the scores as precise measurements. The numbers are rough impressions, not data.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. List the areas that matter to you, for example work, health, money, family, friends, learning, fun, home.
2. Rate each from 1 to 10 on how satisfied you are right now, not how well you are doing.
3. Draw it as a circle or a simple list and see where the dips are.
4. Choose one area to nudge and write one action for the next seven days. Leave the others alone.
**7-day trial:** Score the wheel on Day 1. Do the one chosen action on at least three days. On Day 7, rescore only that area and ask what changed. Track: days you did the action, and your before and after score for that area.
**Pairs well with:** Annual review; Goal ladder; Monthly review.
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin. Similar circle exercises appear in coaching traditions.
**Evidence:** Practice only. It is a reflection tool used in coaching and I know of no good evidence on outcomes, though it can help you notice gaps.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Solo.

## Goal ladder
**One line:** Link a long-term aim down through yearly, monthly and weekly steps so today's task connects to something you care about.
**Best for:** Busy-but-adrift days where work feels disconnected from any bigger aim.
**Skip it when:** Your long-term direction is unclear and you would be inventing it, or you use the ladder to avoid starting. Skip the deeper rungs if a one-month goal is all you need.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Write one aim for three to five years out, in a sentence.
2. Write what would need to be true in one year to be on track.
3. Write the one outcome for this month that serves it, then the one for this week.
4. Pick today's task that serves the weekly outcome and do it first.
**7-day trial:** Build the ladder on Day 1. Each morning, name the rung your top task serves. On Day 7, count how many days your top task had a clear link, and cut or rewrite rungs that nothing served. Track: days your top task linked to the ladder, and tasks that served nothing.
**Pairs well with:** Annual theme; Working backwards; Weekly review.
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin.
**Evidence:** Practice only. The idea that linking tasks to meaningful goals aids motivation has some support in goal research, but I know of no direct tests of ladders as a method.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Either.

## Anti-goals
**One line:** Write down what you refuse to optimise, avoid or become, so your goals have limits.
**Best for:** Over-committed people whose goals quietly crowd out health, relationships or values.
**Skip it when:** You are working through something that needs a professional, or you would use the list as a source of dread. It also backfires if it becomes a long list of fears rather than three clear limits.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Write your current top goal.
2. Ask "what would success cost me that I would not accept?" and write two or three answers (for example, "working past 8pm four nights a week").
3. Turn each into a concrete limit you can check ("no work email after 8pm on weekdays").
4. Put the limits next to the goal where you will see them.
**7-day trial:** Set two limits on Day 1. Each evening, mark whether you stayed inside them. On Day 7, review the breaks and ask whether the limit was wrong or the goal needs to change. Track: days within each limit, and what caused a break.
**Pairs well with:** WOOP; Work stop time; Annual theme.
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin.
**Evidence:** Practice only. It is a popular reflection habit, not a tested method.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Solo.

## Milestone mapping
**One line:** Split a long project into three to seven checkpoints, each with a date and a visible "done" test.
**Best for:** Work that runs for weeks or months, where progress is hard to see and deadlines sneak up.
**Skip it when:** The task is small enough to finish in a few sittings, or the plan will change faster than you can update the map. Too many milestones turns the map into a chore.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Write the final deliverable and its due date.
2. List the major stages between now and then as nouns, such as "draft done", "feedback in", "final checked".
3. For each, write a done test someone else could verify and a target date. Add some slack before the final date.
4. Put the dates on your calendar, and write the next action for the first milestone.
**7-day trial:** Build the map on Day 1. Each day, note which milestone your work served. On Day 7, check whether the first milestone is on track and move any date that was a guess. Track: progress on the first milestone, and any date you moved and why.
**Pairs well with:** Working backwards; OKRs; Weekly review.
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin.
**Evidence:** Practice only. It is standard project-management practice and the benefit of proximal subgoals has some support in goal research, but I know of no direct tests of this method.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Either.
