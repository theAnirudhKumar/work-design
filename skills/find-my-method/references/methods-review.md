# Methods: review and reflection

A method only improves if you look back at how it went. These practices are the looking-back part of a system, at scales from a few minutes at the end of a day to a yearly stocktake. They share one pattern: notice what happened, decide one change, and write it down. The risk is the same for all of them: they become a ritual that produces notes and no change, or a way to judge yourself harshly. Keep each review short, end it with one concrete next step, and drop any review that never changes what you do.

## Daily review
**One line:** Spend five to ten minutes at the end of the day to close loops and set up tomorrow.
**Best for:** Days that end with a head full of loose ends, and mornings that start with "what should I be doing?"
**Skip it when:** You already have a reliable shutdown habit, or the review becomes another hour of planning. If you are exhausted, do the two-minute version only.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Look at what you planned today and mark what got done, moved or dropped.
2. Move anything unfinished to a date, or delete it. Do not leave it floating.
3. Capture any loose thoughts into your one inbox.
4. Write tomorrow's top three and the first action for the first one.
**7-day trial:** Do the four steps at a set time each work day, such as just before you stop work. Track: days you completed the review, and how clear you felt starting the next morning, from 1 to 5. On Day 7, keep it if mornings felt clearer.
**Pairs well with:** Work stop time; Weekly review; Wins log.
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin.
**Evidence:** Practice only. Closing-of-day planning is a common habit, and related research on writing down unfinished tasks is suggestive, but I know of no direct tests of the daily review.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Solo.

## Weekly review
**One line:** Once a week, clear your inbox, look at last week and next week, and choose your focus.
**Best for:** Feeling behind, a task list that has grown out of control, or priorities that slide without notice.
**Skip it when:** It turns into an hour of tidying lists with no real decisions. If you cannot spare 30 minutes, run a 10-minute version rather than skipping.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Empty your inboxes and notes into one list.
2. Scan last week: what moved forward, what slipped, what to carry over or drop.
3. Look at the next two weeks of your calendar for fixed commitments and deadlines.
4. Choose three outcomes for the coming week and place them in specific time slots.
**7-day trial:** Do the review once, at a fixed slot such as Friday afternoon or Sunday evening. Over the following week, notice how often you knew what to do next. Track: whether you completed the three outcomes, and what the review caught that you would have missed.
**Pairs well with:** Daily review; Monthly review; Milestone mapping.
**Comes from:** Folk practice. A weekly review is a core part of David Allen's Getting Things Done, which popularised it.
**Evidence:** Practice only. It is very widely recommended, but I know of little direct testing.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Either.

## Monthly review
**One line:** Once a month, step back to check whether your goals, habits and commitments still match what you want.
**Best for:** Drift between daily work and bigger aims, and recurring tasks that quietly pile up.
**Skip it when:** You are already running a weekly review that covers this, or you tend to turn it into big plans that never happen. Do not let it replace the weekly one.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Block 30 to 45 minutes on a fixed day, such as the last Friday.
2. List the month's main results and what did not happen.
3. Review your goals and recurring commitments. Mark each as keep, change or drop.
4. Choose one change for next month and write the date you will start it.
**7-day trial:** Run a one-time monthly review on Day 1 using the last 30 days. For the next six days, act on the one change you chose. On Day 7, ask whether the review identified something you would have missed. Track: the change you chose, and whether you acted on it.
**Pairs well with:** Weekly review; Wheel of Life; OKRs.
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin.
**Evidence:** Practice only. It is a common habit in goal and project systems, and I know of no direct testing.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Either.

## Annual review
**One line:** Once a year, look back at the whole year and decide what to keep, stop and start.
**Best for:** A year that blurred together, a major transition, or a need for direction for the next twelve months.
**Skip it when:** You are in the middle of a crisis or grief, where a harsh look-back may not help. Also skip making it a long ritual with dozens of questions, which can end up as a document nobody reads.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Scan your calendar and photos from the year and list the ten moments that stand out, good or hard.
2. Write what went well, what did not, and what surprised you.
3. Ask what you would keep, stop and start, and write one answer to each.
4. Pick one theme or priority for the coming year and put a date to check it in three months.
**7-day trial:** Use the first three days to gather material and write, spending 20 minutes each. On Day 4 choose your keep, stop and start. On Day 7, put the first action on your calendar. Track: the decisions made, and whether the first action was scheduled.
**Pairs well with:** Annual theme; Wheel of Life; Monthly review.
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin.
**Evidence:** Practice only. Reflection on experience is supported in general, but I know of no direct tests of an annual review.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Either.

## Personal retrospective
**One line:** Look back on a project, a quarter or a rough patch with a short structure: what happened, why, and what to change.
**Best for:** After a project ends or a stretch that went badly, when you want to learn rather than just move on.
**Skip it when:** The event is too fresh or painful, or you would use it to blame yourself. It backfires when it ends in a long list of faults with no action.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Name the period or event and write a short timeline of what happened.
2. Write what went well, what did not, and what you would do differently.
3. For each thing that did not go well, ask what in the system or situation contributed, not just what you did wrong.
4. Choose one change and write the first action or rule it implies.
**7-day trial:** Run a retrospective on one recent project on Day 1. Act on your chosen change in the following week. On Day 7, note whether it was the right change. Track: the change you chose, and whether you applied it.
**Pairs well with:** Start-stop-continue; Wins log; Monthly review.
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin; retrospectives are standard in software teams.
**Evidence:** Some. A meta-analysis of structured team and individual debriefs found performance gains, though results depend on how they are run, and personal retrospectives like this one have been studied much less.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Either.

## Start-stop-continue
**One line:** List what to start, stop and continue doing, then pick one from each.
**Best for:** A quick, low-effort look at your habits or a team's way of working, with no need to prepare.
**Skip it when:** The list grows without decisions, or in a team where people do not feel safe being honest. It also fails if you choose too many changes at once.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Draw three columns: start, stop, continue.
2. Fill each with whatever comes to mind about the last month. Aim for at least three per column.
3. Circle one item in each column that would matter most and be easiest to do.
4. Turn each circled item into a specific action with a date.
**7-day trial:** Fill the three columns on Day 1 and act on your three circled items. Each evening, tick them off. On Day 7, decide whether to keep each change. Track: which of the three you did each day, and one thing that got in the way.
**Pairs well with:** Personal retrospective; Monthly review; Weekly review.
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin.
**Evidence:** Practice only. It is a common facilitation format, and I know of no direct testing.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Either.

## Wins log
**One line:** Write down what went well, even small things, as they happen or at the end of each day.
**Best for:** Feeling you never make progress, or needing evidence for a review, a raise, a job application or a bad day.
**Skip it when:** It becomes bragging or box-ticking, or you feel pressure to find something every day. Silence on a hard day is fine.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Create one place for it: a note, a document or a notebook that you will open daily.
2. Write down three things from today that went well or that you finished, however small.
3. Add one line about your part in each.
4. Set a reminder at the same time every day.
**7-day trial:** Add one to three entries daily for seven days. On Day 7, read them all and notice patterns: what energised you, what you underrate. Track: days you wrote entries, and one pattern you spotted.
**Pairs well with:** Daily review; Annual review; Weekly review.
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin.
**Evidence:** Some. Related research on writing down positive events, such as gratitude exercises, suggests small wellbeing benefits, but the wins log itself has not been directly tested.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Solo.

## Time audit
**One line:** Record where your time actually goes for a few days, then compare it with where you think it goes.
**Best for:** "Where did the day go?" and finding the gap between intended and actual use of time.
**Skip it when:** Recording stresses you out or takes more effort than the insight is worth, or your days are so fixed that there is nothing to change. It is for noticing, not for judging yourself.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Pick a method that costs nothing: a notes page, a spreadsheet, or a phone alarm that asks "what am I doing?" every hour.
2. Choose simple categories, such as deep work, meetings, email and chat, admin, travel, personal.
3. Start logging now, noting each block as it ends, to the nearest 15 minutes.
4. Plan 15 minutes at the end of the audit to total each category.
**7-day trial:** Log your time for the full week, including at least a few weekend hours. On Day 7, total the categories and compare with your guess. Track: hours per category, and the biggest surprise. Choose one change.
**Pairs well with:** Energy audit; Weekly review; Monthly review.
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin.
**Evidence:** Practice only. Time diaries are used in research on how people spend time, but I know of no direct tests that auditing your own time improves it.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Solo.

## Morning pages
**One line:** Write three pages of longhand, stream-of-consciousness, first thing in the morning, without editing.
**Best for:** A cluttered mind, creative blocks, or a need to get worries on paper before starting the day.
**Skip it when:** It feeds worry instead of settling it, you have no 20 to 30 minutes in the morning, or you are working through trauma or distress, where a professional is the better support. Do not make it a guilt trip if you skip days.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Get a notebook and pen and place them where you will find them in the morning.
2. Set a timer for 10 minutes now, or write until you have filled one page.
3. Write whatever comes, without stopping, correcting or reading back.
4. Close the notebook. Do not review it today.
**7-day trial:** Write a set length each morning before checking your phone, building up to three pages if you like. Each day, rate your mind's clutter on waking and after writing, from 1 to 5. Track: days you wrote, and your before and after clutter ratings.
**Pairs well with:** Daily review; Wins log; Consistent wake time.
**Comes from:** Julia Cameron, in her book The Artist's Way (1992).
**Evidence:** Practice only. Expressive and reflective writing has been studied in other forms, but I know of no direct tests of morning pages as described.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Solo.
