# Methods: habits and routines

These methods help an action become part of ordinary life instead of a daily decision. How long a habit takes to form varies a lot between people and between habits; there is no fixed number of days, so judge by whether the action is getting easier, not by a countdown. Most methods here shrink the action, attach it to a cue, or change the surroundings. Pick one habit at a time. These are general practices, not medical advice; for sleep, health or mental health concerns, follow a professional's advice.

## Tiny Habits
**One line:** Pick a behaviour so small it takes under a minute, tie it to an existing moment, and celebrate it right after.
**Best for:** Starting a habit you have failed at before because it was too big (flossing, stretching, journalling, tidying).
**Skip it when:** You already do the behaviour easily and need more volume, or you want it to stay tiny for ever and never grow.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Choose one outcome you want and shrink it to a version that takes under a minute (floss one tooth, write one line).
2. Find an anchor, something you already do daily: pouring coffee, locking the front door.
3. Write the recipe: "After I [anchor], I will [tiny behaviour]."
4. Pick a quick celebration (a smile, a fist pump, "nice") and do it right after.
5. Practise the recipe once now.
**7-day trial:** Do the tiny behaviour after the anchor each day and celebrate. Track: done or not each day, and how easy it felt (1 to 5). Only grow it if it feels automatic, and by a small amount.
**Pairs well with:** Habit stacking, Minimum viable habit, Habit tracking
**Comes from:** BJ Fogg, a behaviour scientist at Stanford, who developed the method and described it in his 2019 book Tiny Habits.
**Evidence:** Practice only. The ideas build on research on cues and small steps, but the method itself has had little independent testing.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Solo

## Habit stacking
**One line:** Attach a new habit to a specific habit you already do, in the form "After I [current habit], I will [new habit]."
**Best for:** Adding one small behaviour to a day that already has reliable routines.
**Skip it when:** Your current routines are irregular (shift work, travel), so the anchor does not occur predictably, or the anchor is something you do many times a day.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. List five things you do every day without fail (brew coffee, brush teeth, sit down at your desk).
2. Pick the anchor that is closest in time and place to where the new habit fits.
3. Write the stack: "After I [anchor], I will [new habit, under two minutes]."
4. Put a physical reminder at the anchor (a note on the kettle).
5. Do the stack once now.
**7-day trial:** Run one stack each day. Track: did the anchor happen, did the new habit follow. If the anchor was missed several times, switch to a steadier anchor.
**Pairs well with:** Tiny Habits, Implementation intentions, Environment design
**Comes from:** Builds on BJ Fogg's anchoring idea and on if-then planning. The name was popularised by S. J. Scott's 2014 book Habit Stacking and then by James Clear's Atomic Habits (2018).
**Evidence:** Practice only. Direct tests of stacking as a named method are scarce; it is an application of if-then planning (see Implementation intentions), which has stronger support.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Solo

## Two-day rule
**One line:** Never skip a habit two days in a row; one miss is allowed, a second consecutive one is not.
**Best for:** People who drop a habit entirely after a single missed day.
**Skip it when:** The habit needs real rest days (hard exercise), or you have an illness or injury; rest is not a failure.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Pick a habit you want to keep.
2. Write the rule: "I may miss one day, never two."
3. Define the minimum version you will do on a day you are tired or busy.
4. Put a reminder for the day after any miss.
5. Decide now what you will say to yourself after a miss: "One miss, back today."
**7-day trial:** Follow your habit as usual and apply the rule to any miss. Track: misses, and whether you came back the next day. A week with one miss and a return is a success.
**Pairs well with:** Minimum viable habit, Don't break the chain, Habit tracking
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin; popularised by writers on habits.
**Evidence:** Practice only. Sensible and widely used, but not tested directly. One study of habit formation found that missing a single day did not materially disrupt it, while frequent misses did, which is the logic behind it.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Solo

## Environment design
**One line:** Change your surroundings so the habit is easy to do and the competing behaviour is harder.
**Best for:** Habits that fail because of friction (the gym bag is in the car, the phone is on the desk, the fruit is in a drawer).
**Skip it when:** You live or work in a space you cannot change, or the barrier is not the environment but a cause such as stress or unclear goals.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Name the habit and the step that most often stops you.
2. Remove one step of friction: put the item in sight, lay out the clothes, open the book on the pillow.
3. Add friction to one competing behaviour: log out, move the app off the home screen, put the snacks up high.
4. Place a cue where you will see it at the right moment.
5. Check it from the point of view of tomorrow morning.
**7-day trial:** Make three small changes (one for the habit, one against the competing behaviour, one cue) and leave them in place all week. Track: how many days the habit happened, and whether it took less effort than usual.
**Pairs well with:** Habit stacking, Implementation intentions, Tiny Habits
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin. It draws on psychology research on how context cues shape behaviour.
**Evidence:** Some. Research on context, cues and friction supports the mechanism, though effects vary by person and habit.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Either

## Keystone habits
**One line:** Focus on one habit that tends to pull other good habits along with it, such as regular exercise, a weekly review or a fixed bedtime.
**Best for:** Feeling overwhelmed by many things to fix and wanting to start with the one that may help the others.
**Skip it when:** You cannot identify a habit that clearly leads to others for you, or you use the search for the perfect keystone to delay starting anything.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. List three to five things you wish went better (energy, spending, focus, mood, tidiness).
2. For each, ask: which one habit, when it goes well, has made the others easier in the past?
3. Pick the candidate and write its tiny version.
4. Anchor it to an existing cue and do it once now.
5. Write one sentence on what else you hope it will affect, to check later.
**7-day trial:** Do the keystone habit daily in its smallest version. Track: done or not, and each evening one yes/no on whether the other area you chose went any better. Treat a week as a first look, not proof.
**Pairs well with:** Tiny Habits, Habit tracking, Identity-based habits
**Comes from:** Charles Duhigg, who popularised the term in his 2012 book The Power of Habit.
**Evidence:** Practice only. The idea is intuitive and illustrated by stories, but whether a specific habit spreads to others is not well tested.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Solo

## Minimum viable habit
**One line:** Define the smallest version of your habit that still counts, and do it on days when the full version is impossible.
**Best for:** Habits that disappear when life gets busy, tired or chaotic.
**Skip it when:** You always do the minimum and never the full version, and the minimum is no longer pushing you towards your goal.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Write the full version of the habit (30-minute run, 20 minutes of writing).
2. Write the minimum version, which takes under five minutes (put on shoes and walk round the block, write one paragraph).
3. Decide the rule: on bad days, the minimum counts fully.
4. Decide what triggers "bad day": under 6 hours of sleep, a late shift, travel.
5. Put both versions on one note where you see it.
**7-day trial:** Do the full version on easy days and the minimum on hard days; both count. Track: which version you did, and any day you did neither. Check on day 7 how many hard days still had a habit.
**Pairs well with:** Two-day rule, Tiny Habits, Don't break the chain
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin.
**Evidence:** Practice only. Common advice for keeping routines through disruption, with little direct testing.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Solo

## Habit tracking
**One line:** Record each time you do a habit, so you can see how often you do it and adjust.
**Best for:** People who find a visible record motivating, or who want facts instead of guesses about how consistent they are.
**Skip it when:** Tracking becomes a chore or a source of guilt, or the habit is private and the record adds pressure.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Pick one to three habits only.
2. Choose a medium you will actually see: paper grid, wall calendar, notes app, a simple spreadsheet.
3. Define a "done" for each in one line.
4. Record at the moment you finish, not at the end of the day.
5. Add a weekly 5-minute look at the record on a fixed day.
**7-day trial:** Record every day. Track: the number of days done per habit and any pattern in the days missed (a day of the week, a trigger). Use the pattern to change the plan, not to judge yourself.
**Pairs well with:** Don't break the chain, Two-day rule, Tiny Habits
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin.
**Evidence:** Some. A large pooled review of randomised studies, mostly on health goals, found that monitoring progress helped people reach goals, more so when it was written down; effects are modest and vary.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Solo

## Morning routine
**One line:** A short, fixed sequence of actions you do after waking, so the start of the day does not rely on decisions.
**Best for:** Days that begin in a scramble, or people who want a steady launch before work, study or the family wakes.
**Skip it when:** Your mornings are set by children, shifts or health needs, or the routine is so long that one disruption makes you drop it. Do not build it by cutting sleep.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Write what a good first 30 minutes would include, from your own needs (water, light, movement, a plan check).
2. Cut it to three actions that fit in 15 minutes.
3. Put them in order and attach the first to waking ("After I turn off my alarm...").
4. Prepare the night before: clothes, notebook, kettle.
5. Write a one-line short version for rushed days.
**7-day trial:** Do the three-action routine each morning, short version when rushed. Track: days completed and how the first hour of work or study felt (1 to 5). Change one action if it does not help.
**Pairs well with:** Habit stacking, Evening routine, Minimum viable habit
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin.
**Evidence:** Practice only. Many people like routines, but there is little direct evidence for any particular morning routine; what works varies a lot by person.
**Effort to set up:** Medium.   **Works for:** Solo

## Evening routine
**One line:** A short sequence at the end of the day that closes your work, tidies up for tomorrow and winds you down.
**Best for:** Work that spills into the night, a head full of loose ends, or a tomorrow that starts with a hunt for things.
**Skip it when:** Your evenings are controlled by shifts or caring duties and cannot be predicted; use a smaller closing ritual. This is general wellbeing practice, not a treatment for sleep problems; see a professional for those.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Write three things that would make tomorrow easier (next day's top task, bag packed, desk cleared).
2. Add a closing line for work: write what is unfinished and where you will pick it up.
3. Add one wind-down action that is not a screen task, such as reading or a shower.
4. Set a time to start, with a reminder.
5. Do the closing step for work tonight.
**7-day trial:** Run the routine each evening, 15 minutes at most. Track: days done, how long your mind kept working on tasks after finishing (short, medium, long), and how the first 10 minutes of next morning went.
**Pairs well with:** Morning routine, Habit stacking, Habit tracking
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin.
**Evidence:** Practice only. Common advice, with some research on mentally detaching from work and on planning, but little direct testing of a specific routine.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Solo

## Identity-based habits
**One line:** Frame a habit as something a person like you does ("I am someone who moves daily"), then cast small votes for that identity.
**Best for:** Habits you start for a goal and drop once motivation fades, or when "I'm not a gym person" is in the way.
**Skip it when:** The identity label sets a standard you cannot meet and one lapse becomes "I'm a fraud", or when you need a practical fix first, such as less friction.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Write the outcome you want (run a 10K) and the kind of person who would achieve it (a runner).
2. Rewrite the identity as a modest, true-enough statement ("I am becoming someone who keeps promises to myself").
3. List three tiny actions that count as evidence (lace up the shoes, walk 10 minutes, log it).
4. Do one now and notice the evidence.
5. Choose a habit tracker or note for logging them.
**7-day trial:** Do at least one evidence action daily and write one line each evening: "Today I acted like [identity] when...". Track: days with evidence, and how the statement felt (true, partly, not yet).
**Pairs well with:** Tiny Habits, Habit tracking, Keystone habits
**Comes from:** James Clear popularised it in Atomic Habits; the underlying idea of self-image shaping behaviour is much older.
**Evidence:** Practice only. The link between identity and behaviour has research behind it, but this habit method is not well tested.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Solo
