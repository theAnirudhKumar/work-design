# Methods: learning and knowledge

These methods are for taking in material and keeping it: exams, certifications, a new job's vocabulary, a language, a codebase, a clinical protocol. The best-supported ones (spaced repetition, active recall) work on how memory behaves, so they suit almost any subject that has facts or procedures to retain. Learning styles (visual, auditory and so on) are not a method here, because matching teaching to a preferred style has not held up in testing. Evidence labels are conservative: where a method is popular but lightly tested, the card says so.

## Spaced repetition
**One line:** Review each item at growing gaps (a day, a few days, a week, a month) instead of cramming it once.
**Best for:** Facts, vocabulary, formulas, drug names, anything you must still know in three months.
**Skip it when:** The goal is understanding a process or building a skill, not retaining discrete items; or the exam is tomorrow and there is no time to space anything.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Pick one topic and write 10 question-and-answer cards, on paper or in any flashcard app.
2. Test yourself on all 10 now, answering aloud or in writing before flipping.
3. Put cards you got right in a "later" pile (next review in 3 days); wrong ones in a "tomorrow" pile.
4. Write the next review dates on a sticky note or in your calendar.
**7-day trial:** Add 5 to 10 new cards a day and review whatever is due, moving correct cards to longer gaps and wrong ones back to tomorrow. Track: minutes spent per day, and the share of due cards you answered correctly on day 7 versus day 2.
**Pairs well with:** Active recall; Deliberate practice; Interleaving
**Comes from:** Research on the spacing effect goes back to Hermann Ebbinghaus's 1885 memory experiments; the Leitner box system is a later practical scheme from Sebastian Leitner's 1972 book So lernt man lernen.
**Evidence:** Strong. Spacing out review beats massed study for long-term retention in many controlled studies across subjects and ages.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Solo

## Active recall
**One line:** Pull the answer out of your head before checking it, instead of re-reading.
**Best for:** Studying from notes or a textbook when re-reading feels productive but nothing sticks.
**Skip it when:** You are meeting the material for the first time and know nothing yet; read or watch once first, then test.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Read one page or section, then close the book.
2. On blank paper, write everything you remember, or answer three questions you set yourself.
3. Open the book and mark what you missed or got wrong, in a different colour.
4. Turn each gap into a question for next time.
**7-day trial:** For each study session, spend at least half the time testing yourself (blank-page recall, practice questions, explaining aloud) rather than re-reading. Track: how many items you recalled unaided at the start of each session, and minutes spent re-reading versus testing.
**Pairs well with:** Spaced repetition; Feynman technique; Cornell notes
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin; the research literature calls it retrieval practice.
**Evidence:** Strong. Retrieval practice improves later recall compared with re-study in many controlled studies, including classroom ones.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Solo

## Feynman technique
**One line:** Explain the idea in plain words as if teaching a beginner, and study wherever your explanation breaks down.
**Best for:** Concepts you think you understand but cannot yet explain: a statistics test, a legal doctrine, how a system works.
**Skip it when:** The material is pure memorisation (dates, codes), or you have no way to check your explanation against a reliable source.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Write the topic at the top of a blank page.
2. Explain it in simple language, no jargon, as if to a curious 12-year-old.
3. Circle every spot where you hesitated, hand-waved, or used a term you cannot define.
4. Go back to the source for only those gaps, then rewrite the explanation.
**7-day trial:** Do one topic a day, 15 to 20 minutes. Say the final version aloud to a person, a pet, or a voice memo. Track: number of gaps found per topic, and whether you could explain it again two days later.
**Pairs well with:** Active recall; Learning in public; Cornell notes
**Comes from:** Named after the physicist Richard Feynman, but the four-step routine appears to be a later popularisation; I found no source in which he set it out as a method, so the attribution is disputed.
**Evidence:** Some. Explaining material to yourself or others has supportive research (self-explanation, teaching effects), but the named routine has not been tested as a package.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Solo

## Interleaving
**One line:** Mix different problem types or topics within one session instead of finishing one block before starting the next.
**Best for:** Maths, physics, coding exercises, clinical scenarios, any skill where you must pick the right approach, not just apply the one you just saw.
**Skip it when:** You are brand new to a topic and need to learn the basics first; mixing too early feels chaotic and can overwhelm.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Take practice problems from three related topics (say A, B, C).
2. Shuffle them so no two of the same type sit next to each other.
3. For each problem, first write which type it is and why, then solve it.
4. Note which types you confuse; those are your weak spots.
**7-day trial:** In each practice session, mix at least two or three topics rather than blocking by topic. Expect it to feel harder and less fluent. Track: score on a mixed quiz on day 7, and how often you picked the wrong approach on day 2 versus day 7.
**Pairs well with:** Spaced repetition; Active recall; Deliberate practice
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin; studied in cognitive psychology as interleaved practice.
**Evidence:** Some. Studies, mostly on maths and category learning, favour mixing for later test performance, but results depend on the material and the learner's stage.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Solo

## SQ3R
**One line:** A five-step reading routine: Survey, Question, Read, Recite, Review.
**Best for:** Dense textbooks, long reports, or journal articles you must understand and remember, not skim.
**Skip it when:** The text is short, narrative, or already familiar; the routine adds overhead for little gain.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Survey: skim headings, summaries, and figures for two minutes.
2. Question: turn each heading into a question on a note card or margin.
3. Read one section looking for the answer to that question.
4. Recite: close the book and answer the question from memory, aloud or in writing.
5. Review at the end of the chapter by recalling all your questions again.
**7-day trial:** Use it on one chapter or article per day. Track: minutes per section, and how many of your own questions you can answer from memory the next morning.
**Pairs well with:** Active recall; Cornell notes; Spaced repetition
**Comes from:** Francis P. Robinson, in his book Effective Study (first published 1941; the 1946 edition is also often cited).
**Evidence:** Some. Its parts (previewing, self-questioning, recall) have support, but the full routine has little rigorous head-to-head testing.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Solo

## Cornell notes
**One line:** Split each page into a notes column, a cue column, and a summary strip, and use the cues to quiz yourself later.
**Best for:** Lectures, meetings, and lessons where you take notes live and want them to be study-ready.
**Skip it when:** The setting is fast and visual (diagrams, code, whiteboards) and a column layout slows you down; or you never revisit notes, so the cues go unused.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Draw a line about a third from the left and a box across the bottom of a page.
2. During the session, write notes in the large right column.
3. Afterwards, write short questions or keywords in the left column.
4. Write a two-sentence summary in the bottom box.
5. Cover the right column and answer from the cues.
**7-day trial:** Use the layout for every lecture or meeting this week and spend 10 minutes after each filling the cue column and summary. Track: whether you actually did the cover-and-quiz step, and how much you recalled on day 7.
**Pairs well with:** Active recall; SQ3R; Spaced repetition
**Comes from:** Walter Pauk, an education professor at Cornell University, who set it out in his book How to Study in College; Cornell sources place its development in the 1950s.
**Evidence:** Practice only. Widely taught, but the layout itself has little direct testing; the benefit most likely comes from the recall step.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Solo

## Deliberate practice
**One line:** Train a specific weak part of a skill, at the edge of your ability, with fast feedback, instead of repeating what you can already do.
**Best for:** Skills with a clear standard and feedback: an instrument, a language's speaking, surgical or clinical technique, sales calls, a sport.
**Skip it when:** You cannot get feedback, the skill has no clear good-versus-bad, or you are burnt out; it is demanding and not meant for long stretches.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Name one narrow sub-skill you are weakest at (for example, the bar-three transition, or handling price objections).
2. Define what "good" looks like in one sentence and how you will know.
3. Set up feedback: a recording, a teacher, a checklist, or a score.
4. Do 15 minutes of focused reps on only that sub-skill, stopping when attention fades.
**7-day trial:** One focused 20 to 30 minute block a day on a single sub-skill, with feedback each time. Track: the one measurable thing (errors per attempt, tempo reached, score) and your own rating of how hard it felt.
**Pairs well with:** Interleaving; Spaced repetition; Learning in public
**Comes from:** Associated with the psychologist Anders Ericsson and colleagues' studies of expert performers.
**Evidence:** Mixed. Structured, feedback-rich practice is linked to better performance in fields like music and sport, but a later meta-analysis found it explained less of the differences between performers than first claimed (least in professions), and how widely the idea applies is still contested.
**Effort to set up:** Medium.   **Works for:** Solo

## Learning in public
**One line:** Share what you are learning as you go (notes, drafts, questions, small projects) so others can see and respond.
**Best for:** Staying motivated on a long self-directed topic, getting feedback, and building a visible record of progress.
**Skip it when:** Your material is confidential (patient, client, or employer data), or the audience pressure makes you stall; keep it private or share with one trusted person.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Choose one place to post: a blog, a shared doc, a team channel, or a group chat.
2. Write three short sentences: what I studied today, what clicked, what I still do not get.
3. Remove anything confidential or personal before posting.
4. Post it and ask one specific question.
**7-day trial:** Post one short note a day. Track: how many posts you made, and whether any reply taught you something or fixed a misunderstanding.
**Pairs well with:** Feynman technique; Deliberate practice; Active recall
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin; common in software and design communities.
**Evidence:** Practice only. Plausible links to teaching and accountability effects, but little direct testing of the practice itself.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Either
