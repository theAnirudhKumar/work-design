# Methods: starting and beating procrastination

These methods target the gap between knowing what to do and doing it. Most work by making the first move smaller, more specific, or more costly to skip, not by adding willpower. Pick by cause: a task that feels too big needs a smaller start, a task that feels dull needs a pull, and a task you drift away from needs a commitment. None of these fix a task that is unclear, blocked on someone else, or not worth doing.

## Two-minute rule
**One line:** If a task takes under two minutes, do it now; if you are avoiding a bigger one, agree to work on it for just two minutes.
**Best for:** A pile of tiny tasks that clog your head, and big tasks you cannot make yourself begin.
**Skip it when:** You are mid-focus (every small task you do now breaks the deep work), or you use "it's only two minutes" to dodge the one task that matters.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Pick the task you have been avoiding, or open your list of small chores.
2. For a chore: if it takes under two minutes, do it before you do anything else.
3. For an avoided task: set a timer for two minutes and begin, with permission to stop when it rings.
4. When the timer rings, note whether you want to continue. If not, stop and write the next step on the task.
**7-day trial:** Each day, do the two-minute start on one avoided task and clear small chores in one batch of 10 minutes. Track: how many days you started the avoided task, and how many of those you kept going past two minutes.
**Pairs well with:** Tiny first step, Ugly first draft, Implementation intentions
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin. The do-it-now version is associated with David Allen's Getting Things Done; the start-for-two-minutes version was popularised for habits by James Clear.
**Evidence:** Practice only. Widely used, but the two-minute thresholds have not been tested directly.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Solo

## Five-minute rule
**One line:** Work on the task for five minutes, then decide whether to stop or continue.
**Best for:** Dread of starting a task that is probably less painful than it feels.
**Skip it when:** The task has a real blocker (missing info, waiting on a reply); five minutes will only show you the blocker, and you should name it and move on.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Choose the task and write its first visible action in one line (open the file, write the heading).
2. Remove one distraction: phone in another room, other tabs closed.
3. Set a timer for five minutes and work only on that action.
4. At the bell, ask: stop, or five more? Either answer is allowed.
5. If you stop, write the exact next step so tomorrow's start is easier.
**7-day trial:** Use it once a day on the task you most want to avoid. Track: whether you started (yes/no) and whether you kept going after the first five minutes. Expect some days to end at five minutes; that still counts as a start.
**Pairs well with:** Two-minute rule, Implementation intentions, Accountability partner
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin.
**Evidence:** Practice only. Common advice, little direct testing; it rests on the general idea that starting lowers resistance.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Solo

## Swiss cheese method
**One line:** Poke small holes in a big task by doing short, unordered bites of it in spare moments, so the whole thing stops looking solid.
**Best for:** Large, vague projects you cannot face as a whole (a thesis, a tax return, a garage clear-out).
**Skip it when:** The task needs long unbroken stretches (deep writing, complex analysis), or you are using bites as an excuse to avoid ever scheduling the real work.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Write the big task at the top of a page.
2. List 8 to 10 bites that take 5 to 15 minutes each and need no particular order (find the form, list the questions, email the one person).
3. Mark the three easiest.
4. Do one bite now, then cross it off.
5. Keep the list where you will see it during gaps (phone note, sticky note).
**7-day trial:** Do at least two bites a day in gaps such as queues, commutes or after a meeting. Track: bites done per day, and by day 7 whether the project feels smaller (yes/no, plus one line why).
**Pairs well with:** Tiny first step, Five-minute rule, Don't break the chain
**Comes from:** Often credited to Alan Lakein, in his time-management writing of the 1970s.
**Evidence:** Practice only. A sensible use of task decomposition, but this exact technique has not been tested.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Solo

## Temptation bundling
**One line:** Pair something you should do but dread with something you enjoy, and allow the enjoyable thing only during the dreaded one.
**Best for:** Dull, repetitive tasks (chores, admin, exercise, filing) where the problem is no pull, not no time.
**Skip it when:** The task needs your full attention (the treat splits focus), or the treat is something you would do anyway so the rule has no bite.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Pick one dreaded, low-concentration task.
2. Pick a treat you really want and can limit: a podcast, a show, a favourite coffee.
3. Write the rule: "I only get [treat] while I am doing [task]."
4. Set it up now (download the episode, put the headphones by the task).
5. Do the first session today.
**7-day trial:** Use the bundle for the same task on three or four of the seven days; keep the treat off-limits otherwise. Track: sessions done, and whether you actually held to the treat rule.
**Pairs well with:** Commitment devices, Habit stacking, Implementation intentions
**Comes from:** Katy Milkman, a behavioural scientist who named it in research on gym attendance.
**Evidence:** Mixed. An early study reported benefits, but results across studies are not consistent and may depend on the person and the treat.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Solo

## Implementation intentions
**One line:** Decide in advance exactly when, where and how you will act, written as "If [situation], then I will [action]."
**Best for:** Goals you already want to reach but keep failing to act on, because no moment is ever picked.
**Skip it when:** You do not actually want the goal (a plan will not fix that), or the situation cannot be predicted so the "if" never occurs.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Name one specific action toward a goal you care about.
2. Pick a cue you will reliably meet: a time, place or event ("when I sit at my desk after lunch").
3. Write the sentence: "If [cue], then I will [action]." Keep the action small and concrete.
4. Say it aloud or put it where you will see it.
5. Add a second plan for the obstacle you expect: "If [obstacle], then I will [workaround]."
**7-day trial:** Use one if-then plan daily. Track: did the cue happen (yes/no), and did you act when it did. If the cue often does not happen, change the cue, not your effort.
**Pairs well with:** Habit stacking, Tiny first step, Environment design
**Comes from:** Peter Gollwitzer, in psychology research published in 1999.
**Evidence:** Strong. Repeated studies, including reviews that pool them, support if-then planning for helping people act on goals they already hold; effects are moderate, not magic.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Either

## Commitment devices
**One line:** Set up a consequence or restriction now that makes the avoided behaviour easier to do, or the avoidance harder, later.
**Best for:** Planning to do something in the future but reliably choosing otherwise in the moment (saving, studying, screen time).
**Skip it when:** The penalty is harsh enough to cause stress or shame, or you will simply turn the device off when tempted.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Name the exact behaviour you want and the moment you usually give in.
2. Choose a device: a site blocker, booking a class or session in advance, a refundable deposit with a friend, a deadline given to someone else.
3. Make it hard to undo in the moment (a friend holds the password, the deposit is not returned).
4. Set it up now and confirm it works.
5. Keep the cost small and fair so you will keep using the method.
**7-day trial:** Run one device all week. Track: days you kept to the commitment, and any time you bypassed it (and how). If you bypass it, make it harder, not harsher.
**Pairs well with:** Accountability partner, Temptation bundling, Environment design
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin. The idea is old and is studied under the same name in behavioural economics.
**Evidence:** Some. Studies in saving, health and study settings show benefits, mostly in specific contexts, and effects depend on the design.
**Effort to set up:** Medium.   **Works for:** Either

## Ugly first draft
**One line:** Produce a deliberately rough version fast, with permission for it to be bad, then improve it.
**Best for:** Blank-page paralysis and perfectionism on writing, design, slides, code or plans.
**Skip it when:** The work is safety-critical or a mistake is costly and hard to undo, or you are using "draft" to avoid ever finishing.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Set a timer for 10 minutes.
2. Write or build the whole thing at low quality: skeleton headings, placeholder text, [fix later] marks.
3. Do not edit, delete or look things up; put gaps in brackets and carry on.
4. When the timer rings, save it under the name "draft 0".
5. Schedule the improving pass for a separate session.
**7-day trial:** Make one ugly first draft per day for the piece you are working on (or a new small piece each day). Track: minutes to first draft and whether the later improving pass felt easier than starting fresh.
**Pairs well with:** Five-minute rule, Tiny first step, Don't break the chain
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin. Anne Lamott popularised the idea for writers in her book Bird by Bird.
**Evidence:** Practice only. Widely taught by writers and designers, little direct testing.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Solo

## Tiny first step
**One line:** Shrink the start of a task until it is so small that refusing feels silly, such as opening the document or putting on your shoes.
**Best for:** Tasks that feel too big, vague or heavy to begin.
**Skip it when:** The task is unclear (you need a decision first) or you already start easily and need a better schedule, not a smaller step.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Write the task you are avoiding.
2. Ask: what is the smallest physical action that counts as starting? (open the file, lay out the kit, write one sentence)
3. If it takes longer than two minutes, shrink it again.
4. Do that step now.
5. Write the step after it, so the next session has an obvious entry point.
**7-day trial:** Each day, pick your most avoided task, name its tiny first step and do it. Track: whether you did the step (yes/no) and whether you did more afterwards. Do not judge days when you only did the step.
**Pairs well with:** Two-minute rule, Implementation intentions, Swiss cheese method
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin.
**Evidence:** Practice only. A plausible mechanism, widely used, little direct testing.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Solo

## Don't break the chain
**One line:** Mark each day you do the task on a calendar and try not to leave a gap in the row of marks.
**Best for:** A daily action you want to keep going, such as writing, practising or exercising, where seeing progress motivates you.
**Skip it when:** Missing a day makes you give up entirely, or the task should not be daily (rest matters for exercise and for recovery).
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Choose one action that takes 15 minutes or less and can be done on a bad day.
2. Define what counts as done, in a sentence.
3. Put a wall or paper calendar (or an app) where you see it daily.
4. Do the action today and mark it.
5. Write a rule for a miss now: "If I miss a day, I restart the next day, no catching up."
**7-day trial:** Mark each day you do it. Track: the length of your chain and, if broken, how quickly you restarted. The restart matters more than the streak.
**Pairs well with:** Habit tracking, Two-day rule, Minimum viable habit
**Comes from:** Often attributed to the comedian Jerry Seinfeld, who is said to have used a wall calendar for writing jokes; the attribution is disputed.
**Evidence:** Practice only. Widely used, little direct testing; seeing progress helps some people, and a broken streak discourages others.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Solo

## Accountability partner
**One line:** Tell another person what you will do and by when, and report back to them.
**Best for:** Tasks where you let yourself down easily but would not let someone else down.
**Skip it when:** The other person judges harshly or does not follow up, or the pressure turns into avoidance.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Choose a person who will check in without judging: a friend, colleague or study partner.
2. Send a message with the task, the deadline and how you will report ("Draft of section 2 by Friday 5pm, I will send you the file").
3. Ask them to reply to your report, even with a thumbs-up.
4. Agree what happens if you miss it (just say so, and set a new date).
5. Put the check-in time in your calendar.
**7-day trial:** Send one commitment and one report this week. Track: whether you reported on time, and whether the work got done earlier than it otherwise would. Swap roles if they also want a partner.
**Pairs well with:** Commitment devices, Implementation intentions, Don't break the chain
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin.
**Evidence:** Some. Studies on social support and goal commitment lean positive, but results vary by person and by how the partner behaves.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Either
