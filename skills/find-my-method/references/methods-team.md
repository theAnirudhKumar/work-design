# Methods: working with others

These methods change how a team or a pair coordinates: when people talk, who decides, who does what, and what gets written down. Most are team conventions with little direct research behind them, and the evidence labels say so. Each one can backfire when adopted as ritual, so every card says when to skip it. Try one at a time, with agreement from the people affected, and judge it after a week against something you can observe.

## Async-first
**One line:** Default to written updates and decisions that people answer when ready, and reserve live calls for what truly needs them.
**Best for:** Teams across time zones, shift patterns, or with heavy meeting loads and little uninterrupted time.
**Skip it when:** The work is urgent, emotional, or ambiguous and needs fast back-and-forth; or people do not read what is written, so silence replaces decisions.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Pick one recurring meeting or interruption that is mostly information passing.
2. Write a template for the update (status, blockers, decision needed, deadline for replies).
3. Tell the team the reply window, such as "answer by end of next day".
4. Replace one occurrence of the meeting with the written version.
**7-day trial:** Run the chosen item async for the week. Track: replies received within the window, and any decision that stalled or needed a call after all.
**Pairs well with:** Decision log; Working agreements; Meeting-free days
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin; common in distributed and remote teams.
**Evidence:** Practice only. Widely used, with little direct testing of async-versus-meeting trade-offs.
**Effort to set up:** Medium.   **Works for:** Team

## Meeting-free days
**One line:** Reserve one or more weekdays with no internal meetings so people get unbroken work time.
**Best for:** Teams whose focus work keeps being chopped up by calls, such as engineers, writers, analysts, or designers.
**Skip it when:** Your role is service or customer facing and cannot pause, or the meetings simply pile onto the other days and make them worse.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Propose one day (many teams pick a midweek day) to your manager or team.
2. Agree what counts as an exception: customer emergencies, incidents, external calls.
3. Block the day on the shared calendar with a clear title.
4. Move or cancel any meetings already booked on it.
**7-day trial:** Hold one meeting-free day this week. Track: hours of uninterrupted work you got, how many exceptions were made, and whether other days got more crowded.
**Pairs well with:** Async-first; Working agreements; Directly responsible individual
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin.
**Evidence:** Practice only. Many teams report benefits, but controlled testing is sparse and effects likely depend on how meetings are rescheduled.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Team

## Daily standup
**One line:** A short, regular check-in where each person says what they are doing and where they need help.
**Best for:** Small teams working on tightly linked tasks where blockers appear quickly and people need to stay aligned.
**Skip it when:** It turns into status reporting to a manager, runs long, or the team already shares progress elsewhere; then it is a daily cost with no payoff.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Fix a time and a cap, say 10 to 15 minutes, standing or on a call.
2. Use three prompts: what I am working on today, what is in my way, who I need.
3. Park any discussion: note it and hold it after with only the people involved.
4. Have one person note blockers and who owns the follow-up.
**7-day trial:** Run it daily for five working days. Track: how many blockers surfaced that would otherwise have waited, and whether it ran over its cap.
**Pairs well with:** Working agreements; Decision log; Async-first
**Comes from:** Associated with Scrum and agile software teams; no single originator. The Scrum Guide caps the Daily Scrum at 15 minutes but does not prescribe a format, so the three prompts below are one common choice.
**Evidence:** Practice only. Widely used in software, with little direct testing of its effect.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Team

## RACI
**One line:** For one piece of work, name who is Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, and Informed.
**Best for:** Projects where it is unclear who decides, who does the work, and who needs to know, causing duplicated effort or dropped balls.
**Skip it when:** The team is small or the work is routine; a RACI chart for everything becomes paperwork nobody reads, and it can freeze decisions into approvals.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. List 5 to 8 key tasks or decisions for one project down the side.
2. List the people or roles across the top.
3. Mark each cell R, A, C, I, or blank, with exactly one A per row.
4. Check that no row has two As and none has zero.
5. Share it with those named and ask for corrections.
**7-day trial:** Use it for one project only. Track: how many times someone asked "who owns this?" during the week, and any row people disagreed on.
**Pairs well with:** Directly responsible individual; Decision log; Delegation levels
**Comes from:** Folk practice; it descends from responsibility assignment matrices (also called linear responsibility charts), and accounts of its origin conflict, so there is no single verified originator.
**Evidence:** Practice only. Common in project management, with little direct testing of its effect on outcomes.
**Effort to set up:** Medium.   **Works for:** Team

## Decision log
**One line:** Keep a simple running list of decisions made, with the date, owner, reason, and what was ruled out.
**Best for:** Teams that keep relitigating old decisions, or where new joiners ask "why did we do it this way?"
**Skip it when:** Nobody will write or read it, or decisions are trivial and reversible; a log that is not kept up to date misleads.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Create one shared page or table with columns: date, decision, owner, why, alternatives, review date.
2. Add the last three decisions from memory.
3. Agree who updates it and when (for example, at the end of any meeting that decides something).
4. Link it from your team channel or project page.
**7-day trial:** Log every decision made this week. Track: number of decisions logged, and any time someone asked about an earlier decision and the log answered it.
**Pairs well with:** Async-first; RACI; Directly responsible individual
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin.
**Evidence:** Practice only. Used in engineering and project work, with little direct testing.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Either

## Directly responsible individual
**One line:** Every task or project has exactly one named person who is answerable for it, even if many people help.
**Best for:** Work that gets stuck because everyone assumed someone else would handle it.
**Skip it when:** The named person lacks the authority or time to do it; naming them then just assigns blame.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. List three current items with unclear ownership.
2. For each, ask "who will make sure this happens?" and get one name, not a team.
3. Confirm with that person, and agree what they can decide alone.
4. Write the name next to the item where everyone sees it.
**7-day trial:** Assign one owner to every item on a shared list for the week. Track: items with no owner at start versus end, and how many stalled items moved once owned.
**Pairs well with:** RACI; Delegation levels; Decision log
**Comes from:** Often associated with Apple, where Adam Lashinsky described it in Inside Apple (2012); the exact origin is unclear.
**Evidence:** Practice only. Popular in product teams, with little direct testing.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Team

## Working agreements
**One line:** A short list of rules the team writes together about how it communicates, meets, and hands work over.
**Best for:** New teams, teams with friction over response times, meeting etiquette, or after-hours messages.
**Skip it when:** It is imposed by a manager with no input, or no one revisits it; unowned rules are ignored.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Ask each person for one thing that would help the team work better.
2. Group them into 5 to 7 plain-language rules (for example, "urgent means phone, not chat").
3. Agree out loud that everyone accepts them.
4. Post the list somewhere visible and set a review date.
**7-day trial:** Adopt the list for a week. Track: how many times a rule was broken or invoked, and which rule people want changed at the review.
**Pairs well with:** Async-first; Daily standup; Meeting-free days
**Comes from:** Associated with agile team practice; no single originator.
**Evidence:** Practice only. Common in agile coaching, with little direct testing.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Team

## Delegation levels
**One line:** Choose how much authority to hand over, from "do as I say" up to full delegation, and name the level out loud.
**Best for:** Managers, team leads, and parents or founders who either micromanage or hand things off vaguely and get burned.
**Skip it when:** The person is new and needs close support, or the task is high-risk and irreversible; start at a lower level and move up.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Pick one decision you are about to hand off. Appelo applies the levels to decision areas, not to every small task.
2. Choose a level: tell them the decision; sell them on it; consult them first; agree together; advise (they decide); inquire after (they decide and you ask afterwards); or full delegation.
3. Tell them the level in plain words, such as "decide, then tell me afterwards".
4. Agree a check-in date and what a good outcome looks like.
**7-day trial:** Use a named level on two handoffs this week. Track: how often you stepped in uninvited, and whether the person said the level was clear.
**Pairs well with:** Directly responsible individual; RACI; Working agreements
**Comes from:** The seven-level version (tell, sell, consult, agree, advise, inquire, delegate) comes from Jurgen Appelo's Management 3.0, where it underpins the Delegation Poker exercise.
**Evidence:** Practice only. Plausible and widely taught, with little direct testing of the levels.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Team
