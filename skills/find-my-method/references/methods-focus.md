# Methods: focus and attention

These twelve methods protect the stretch of uninterrupted attention that hard work needs. Some change what you do (one task at a time), some change your environment (phone out of reach, sites blocked), and some change the calendar (meeting-free blocks). It is well supported in general terms that switching between tasks costs time and accuracy, and that interruptions make the return to a task harder. Beyond that, most of these are practices with little direct testing, and the cards say which. Try one at a time.

## Deep work
**One line:** Schedule long blocks for demanding work with no interruptions, and treat them like appointments.
**Best for:** Writing, coding, studying, analysis and any task that needs sustained thought and keeps getting chopped up.
**Skip it when:** Your job is mostly responding to people in real time (a nurse on a ward, front-desk or on-call work); protect smaller windows instead.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Pick the single task that matters most this week and name the output you want (a draft, a solved problem set).
2. Find a 60 to 90 minute slot in the next two days and put it in your calendar as a booked meeting with yourself.
3. Decide where you will work and what you will close or silence (chat, email tab, phone).
4. Write a first action on a sticky note so you can start without deciding.
**7-day trial:** Hold one deep block a day, shorter on busy days. Track: block minutes held, and whether the named output moved forward.
**Pairs well with:** Monotasking; Phone-away rule; Meeting-free blocks
**Comes from:** Cal Newport, who popularised the term in his book Deep Work.
**Evidence:** Practice only. The idea rests on general findings about attention, but the method itself is not directly tested.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Either

## Monotasking
**One line:** Work on one thing at a time, close everything else, and finish or deliberately pause before switching.
**Best for:** Feeling busy all day with little finished, and many open tabs and half-done tasks.
**Skip it when:** The work is truly parallel by nature, such as monitoring several screens or patients where watching many things is the job.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Write one task on a sticky note and put it where you can see it.
2. Close every window, tab and app not needed for that task.
3. Work on it until you reach a natural stop, and if a new idea or request appears, write it on a side list and go back.
**7-day trial:** For one chosen hour a day, do only the one task. Track: tasks fully finished in that hour, and how many times you caught yourself switching.
**Pairs well with:** Distraction parking lot; Deep work; Phone-away rule
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin.
**Evidence:** Strong. Controlled studies repeatedly show that switching between tasks slows people down and raises errors compared with doing them one at a time; whether a given person gains much from strict monotasking all day is less settled.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Either

## Notification batching
**One line:** Turn off most alerts and check messages at set times, so they come to you in groups instead of one at a time.
**Best for:** A buzzing phone and constant pings that pull you away from whatever you started.
**Skip it when:** Someone depends on you reaching them quickly (a caregiver, on-call duty); keep an exception for those people.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. In phone settings, turn off alerts for every app except calls, texts from a short list of people, and anything urgent.
2. Turn off badges and banners on your computer chat and email.
3. Choose 3 times a day to check messages (for example 10:00, 13:00, 16:00) and put them on a sticky note.
**7-day trial:** Check messages only at the set times. Track: number of check-ins per day, and anything important you missed (usually near zero).
**Pairs well with:** Email batching; Phone-away rule; Inbox zero
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin.
**Evidence:** Some. A few small studies of notification limits and batched checking report benefits for attention or stress, but results vary and the samples are limited.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Either

## Phone-away rule
**One line:** During focus time, the phone is out of sight and out of arm's reach, not just face down.
**Best for:** Reaching for your phone without deciding to, especially during study or demanding work.
**Skip it when:** You need the phone for work (two-factor codes, a child's school calling); set a short list of allowed contacts first.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Pick a place away from your desk: another room, a bag or a drawer.
2. Before your next work block, silence the phone and put it there.
3. Note the time on a sticky note and check the phone only when the block ends.
**7-day trial:** Phone away for every focus block this week. Track: blocks done with the phone out of reach, and how many times you wanted to check it.
**Pairs well with:** Notification batching; Deep work; Monotasking
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin.
**Evidence:** Mixed. Some studies find that a phone merely nearby reduces attention, but later attempts have not always reproduced the effect.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Either

## Website blocking
**One line:** Use a blocker app or browser extension to make distracting sites unreachable during set hours.
**Best for:** Knowing what you should be doing but drifting to news, social media or video sites by habit.
**Skip it when:** Your work needs the open web, or you will simply switch to another device; address the cause of the drifting instead.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. List the 3 sites or apps you most often drift to.
2. Install a blocker for your browser or phone (many are free).
3. Block those sites for your two best working hours tomorrow.
4. Decide in advance that you will not turn the blocker off during those hours.
**7-day trial:** Block the list during one chosen window each day. Track: minutes you tried to open a blocked site (some tools count this) and tasks finished in the window.
**Pairs well with:** Ulysses contract; Deep work; Distraction parking lot
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin.
**Evidence:** Practice only. It is widely used, and related research on removing temptation exists, but blockers themselves have little direct testing.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Solo

## Distraction parking lot
**One line:** Keep a scrap of paper beside you, and write down any urge or stray thought instead of acting on it.
**Best for:** Breaking focus to check something, look up a fact or answer a thought that just popped up.
**Skip it when:** Your interruptions come from other people, not from your own head; use a countermeasure that deals with them directly.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Put a blank sheet or sticky note next to your work.
2. Before starting, write the one task you are working on at the top.
3. Whenever an urge hits ("email Sam", "search that"), write two words on the sheet and return to the task.
4. At the end of the block, read the list and move useful items to your task list.
**7-day trial:** Use the sheet in every work block. Track: items parked per block, and how many turned out to be worth doing.
**Pairs well with:** Monotasking; Deep work; Brain dump
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin.
**Evidence:** Practice only. Writing down intrusive thoughts is a common technique, but its use for focus has not been tested directly.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Solo

## Body doubling
**One line:** Work alongside another person, in the room or on a video call, each doing your own task.
**Best for:** Starting tasks you keep putting off, especially dull admin, and people who focus better with someone else present.
**Skip it when:** The other person talks to you, or your task needs complete quiet or confidential material.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Ask a friend, colleague or classmate: "Can we work quietly together for 45 minutes?"
2. Say aloud, in one sentence, what each of you will work on.
3. Start a timer, keep mics muted or talk minimal, and work.
4. At the end, say what you got done in one sentence each.
**7-day trial:** Do two sessions this week. Track: whether you started the avoided task, and time spent on it compared with a typical solo attempt.
**Pairs well with:** Monotasking; Distraction parking lot
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin; the term is common among ADHD coaches and communities.
**Evidence:** Practice only. Many people report that it helps, but there is little direct research.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Either

## Ulysses contract
**One line:** Make a commitment ahead of time that takes away your ability to give in later, such as handing over passwords or paying a penalty.
**Best for:** A repeated slip you can predict, like late-night scrolling or skipping a study session.
**Skip it when:** The rule is so strict it harms you or others, or you have a real need to override it (an emergency).
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Name the exact behaviour you want to stop ("no phone in bed after 10pm").
2. Pick a barrier you cannot easily undo in the moment: leave the phone charging in the kitchen, give a friend the app password, or set a timer lock.
3. Tell one person your rule and the date it starts.
**7-day trial:** Apply the barrier every day. Track: nights or sessions you kept it, and any time you broke it, with what triggered it.
**Pairs well with:** Website blocking; Phone-away rule
**Comes from:** Named after Ulysses (Odysseus) tying himself to the mast in Homer's Odyssey; the idea is studied in behavioural economics as a commitment device.
**Evidence:** Mixed. Commitment devices help some people in some settings, and the effect depends on the person and the design.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Solo

## Email batching
**One line:** Read and answer email in a few set slots per day instead of keeping it open.
**Best for:** Constant checking, replies that interrupt real work, and an inbox that fills your whole day.
**Skip it when:** Your role is mainly reactive email, such as customer support or a fast-moving client, where slots would be too far apart.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Choose 2 to 3 email times (for example 9:30, 12:30, 16:00), each 20 to 30 minutes.
2. Put them in your calendar.
3. Add a short auto-reply or signature line if useful: "I read email at 9:30, 12:30 and 4. For urgent matters, call me."
4. Close the email app or tab the rest of the time.
**7-day trial:** Use only the slots. Track: number of email check-ins per day, and any complaint about slow replies.
**Pairs well with:** Inbox zero; Notification batching
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin.
**Evidence:** Mixed. A few small studies report lower stress with fewer email checks, while others find no clear difference or effects that depend on the person's job.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Either

## Meeting-free blocks
**One line:** Reserve fixed times in the week, such as mornings or one whole day, when no meetings may be booked.
**Best for:** A calendar full of meetings that leaves no time for your own work.
**Skip it when:** Your schedule is set by others (shifts, class timetables) or the blocks are routinely overridden; start with a smaller window.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Open next week's calendar and find the two longest open stretches.
2. Create recurring events titled "Focus: no meetings" and mark them busy.
3. Tell your manager or team which blocks they are: "I am heads-down Tuesday and Thursday mornings."
4. When a meeting request lands in a block, offer another time.
**7-day trial:** Hold the blocks all week. Track: blocks kept intact, and what you finished in them.
**Pairs well with:** Deep work; Maker's schedule; Notification batching
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin.
**Evidence:** Practice only. Some companies report good results, but there are few controlled tests.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Either

## Maker's schedule
**One line:** If your work needs long stretches to create, divide the week into large blocks, with meetings bunched together rather than scattered.
**Best for:** Programmers, writers, designers and researchers whose day is cut up by one-hour meetings.
**Skip it when:** You manage people and your work is mostly meetings; this is built for makers.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Write the type of work you do in two columns: "needs a long stretch" and "fits in an hour".
2. Look at your usual week and pick half-days (for example mornings) to keep for the first column.
3. Move or propose meetings to the other half-days, back to back.
**7-day trial:** Keep your chosen half-days for making. Track: number of meetings that landed in them, and hours of uninterrupted work.
**Pairs well with:** Meeting-free blocks; Deep work
**Comes from:** Paul Graham, in his essay "Maker's Schedule, Manager's Schedule".
**Evidence:** Practice only. It is an essay and a widely shared idea, not a tested method.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Either

## Shutdown ritual
**One line:** End the workday with a short fixed routine, then stop working until the next day.
**Best for:** Thinking about work all evening, answering messages at night, and days that never feel finished.
**Skip it when:** You work in shifts or have urgent on-call duties; use the closing steps as an end-of-shift routine instead. If work worry affects your sleep or health, follow a professional's advice.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Check your inbox and chat one last time and note any urgent reply.
2. Review tomorrow's calendar and write the top 3 tasks for tomorrow.
3. Move every open loop to a list, so nothing sits in your head.
4. Close tabs and say a fixed phrase aloud ("Shutdown complete").
**7-day trial:** Do the same steps at the same time each workday. Track: days completed, and how often you thought about work after the ritual.
**Pairs well with:** Brain dump; Zen to Done; Deep work
**Comes from:** Cal Newport, who popularised the "shutdown complete" routine; the habit of ending the day with a plan is older and folk.
**Evidence:** Some. Research on mentally detaching from work and on writing a plan before bed is supportive, but it is indirect and does not test this exact routine.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Solo
