# Methods: energy and recovery

These are general wellbeing practices for managing your energy across a workday and week, not medical advice. Anyone with a health condition, an injury, a sleep disorder, an eye condition, a pregnancy or a medication that affects energy should follow their own clinician's guidance over anything here, and should adapt or skip any step that does not suit them. Shift workers, carers and people with fixed schedules will need to translate "morning" and "evening" to their own day. The methods below are small experiments: try one, watch how you feel and perform, and keep it only if it helps you.

## Energy audit
**One line:** For a week, note your energy level a few times a day to find when you are sharp and when you fade.
**Best for:** Not knowing when to schedule hard work, or feeling tired without knowing why.
**Skip it when:** Tracking makes you anxious, or you already know your pattern. If low energy is persistent or worrying, talk to a clinician rather than tracking harder.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Set up a simple note or sheet with columns for time, energy (1 to 5), and what you were doing.
2. Choose three check-in times, such as mid-morning, mid-afternoon and early evening, and set reminders.
3. Do the first check-in now, writing a one-line note on what affected it (sleep, food, a meeting).
4. Decide that on Day 7 you will look for the highest and lowest windows.
**7-day trial:** Log energy at your three check-ins each day. On Day 7, mark the peaks and dips, then move one demanding task into a peak window the following week. Track: your energy score at each check-in, and one thing that seemed to lift or drain it.
**Pairs well with:** Time audit; Micro-breaks; Consistent wake time.
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin.
**Evidence:** Practice only. Self-rating energy is common in coaching and wellbeing, but there is little direct testing of the audit itself, and self-reports are rough.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Solo.

## Micro-breaks
**One line:** Take very short pauses of under five minutes between or within tasks, away from the screen or the task.
**Best for:** Long stretches of focused or repetitive work where attention and mood sag by mid-session.
**Skip it when:** You are deep in a good flow state and the break would only interrupt, or the break turns into phone scrolling that leaves you more drained. Choose breaks that are different from the work.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Pick a break length of one to five minutes.
2. Choose three break activities that are not a screen: stand and stretch, look out a window, refill water, walk to another room.
3. Set a repeating timer for every 30 to 60 minutes, whichever fits your work.
4. When it rings, stop at a natural pause and take the break before checking messages.
**7-day trial:** Use the timer on your work days. Each evening, rate how you felt at the end of the day from 1 to 5. On Day 7, compare with a day you skipped the breaks. Track: number of breaks taken, and your end-of-day rating.
**Pairs well with:** Walking breaks; 20-20-20 rule; Energy audit.
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin.
**Evidence:** Some. Several small studies of short work breaks report benefits for fatigue or mood, but effects are modest, depend on what the break is, and studies vary in quality.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Either.

## Walking breaks
**One line:** Step away from your desk for a short walk, indoors or out, at planned points in the day.
**Best for:** Sitting-heavy days, afternoon slumps, or a stuck problem where a change of scene helps.
**Skip it when:** Walking is painful, unsafe or not possible for you (use another movement that suits you, or follow your clinician's advice), or when weather, location or workplace norms make it unrealistic. A phone call walking is fine if it is not stressful.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Choose one or two times in the day for a walk of 5 to 15 minutes, such as after lunch or mid-afternoon.
2. Choose a route that needs no planning: around the block, the stairs, a corridor, a loop of the office.
3. Tie the walk to something that already happens: right after lunch, or when a meeting ends.
4. Leave your work open and your phone in a pocket on silent.
**7-day trial:** Take your planned walk each day you can. Rate your energy afterwards from 1 to 5. On Day 7, see which walk time gave the best lift. Track: walks taken, and your post-walk energy rating.
**Pairs well with:** Micro-breaks; Rest day; Energy audit.
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin.
**Evidence:** Some. Studies on short bouts of light movement and mood or alertness are generally supportive, though many are small and short-term, and I do not claim any specific health outcome.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Either.

## 20-20-20 rule
**One line:** Every 20 minutes of screen work, look at something about 20 feet (6 metres) away for 20 seconds.
**Best for:** Screen-heavy days with tired, dry or strained eyes.
**Skip it when:** The timer interrupts work that needs unbroken attention, or you have an eye condition or persistent symptoms. See an eye professional for symptoms; this rule is not treatment.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Find a spot you can look at that is far from your screen, such as a window or the far wall.
2. Set a repeating 20-minute timer on your phone or computer.
3. When it rings, look at that spot for 20 seconds and blink a few times.
4. Optionally, use the pause to drop your shoulders and take one slow breath.
**7-day trial:** Run the timer during screen work on your work days. Each evening, rate how your eyes feel from 1 to 5. On Day 7, decide if it helps enough to keep, or switch to a longer interval. Track: days you used it, and the evening eye comfort rating.
**Pairs well with:** Micro-breaks; Walking breaks; Digital sunset.
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin.
**Evidence:** Mixed. It is widely recommended and short breaks may ease reported discomfort, but the exact 20-20-20 timing has little direct testing.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Solo.

## Consistent wake time
**One line:** Get up at about the same time every day, including days off, and build the rest of your schedule around it.
**Best for:** Irregular sleep patterns that leave you groggy or unable to plan a day around your energy.
**Skip it when:** Your schedule is set by shifts, newborn care or illness, or you have a sleep disorder. In those cases follow your clinician's advice and use a wake time that suits your real circumstances.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Choose a wake time you can keep on most days, including weekends, within an hour either way.
2. Set one alarm for it and move the phone out of reach of your bed.
3. Decide on one thing to do soon after waking, such as opening curtains, a glass of water or a short walk outside.
4. Work out roughly when you need to be in bed to have the sleep you usually need, and set a reminder.
**7-day trial:** Wake within your window each day for seven days. Each morning, rate how alert you feel from 1 to 5. On Day 7, see if the pattern is sustainable and adjust the time if not. Track: whether you hit the window, and your morning alertness rating.
**Pairs well with:** Digital sunset; Energy audit; Work stop time.
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin; regular schedules feature in common sleep hygiene advice.
**Evidence:** Some. Regular sleep and wake times are part of mainstream sleep advice and are supported by some research, though effects vary and this is not a treatment for any sleep problem.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Solo.

## Rest day
**One line:** Choose one day, or half a day, a week with no work and no work-related tasks.
**Best for:** Work that bleeds into every day, and a feeling of never being fully off.
**Skip it when:** Your job or caring role makes a full day off impossible. Use a half day or a regular evening instead, and be careful not to pile the chores on top. It also backfires if you spend the day anxious about what you are not doing.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Choose the day or half-day that is easiest to protect, and write it on your calendar.
2. Decide what counts as work: email, chat, planning, errands for the job. Write three things you will not do.
3. Plan one thing you enjoy so the time has a shape.
4. Tell anyone who might expect a reply, and set an auto-response if needed.
**7-day trial:** Protect one rest block this week. Note when you were tempted to check work and whether you did. The next day, rate how ready you feel for work from 1 to 5. Track: whether you stayed off, and your readiness rating the next day.
**Pairs well with:** Work stop time; Weekly review; Walking breaks.
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin; weekly rest days appear in many cultures and traditions.
**Evidence:** Some. Studies on psychological detachment from work during time off generally link it with better recovery, but most are observational, so cause and effect are not settled.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Solo.

## Digital sunset
**One line:** Set a time in the evening after which you put away screens, or at least work screens, to wind down.
**Best for:** Late-night scrolling, work email at bedtime, or trouble switching off.
**Skip it when:** Your phone is your lifeline for caring, on-call or safety duties, or you work evenings. Adjust it to the end of your own day. It will not fix insomnia; see a clinician for that.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Choose a time, for example 60 minutes before you want to be asleep. Pick one you can keep most days.
2. Set a phone alarm labelled with the time, and a second one 10 minutes before as a warning.
3. Pick a charging spot outside the bedroom or away from the bed.
4. Choose two screen-free wind-down things: a book, a shower, tidying, stretching, a conversation.
**7-day trial:** Keep your cut-off each night you can. Each morning, note how you felt on waking from 1 to 5, and how long you took to switch off. On Day 7 see if the pattern helps or if you need a different time. Track: nights you kept the cut-off, and your morning rating.
**Pairs well with:** Consistent wake time; Work stop time; 20-20-20 rule.
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin.
**Evidence:** Mixed. Evening screen use and sleep have been linked in studies, but results vary, and what you do on the screen matters as much as the screen itself.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Solo.

## Work stop time
**One line:** Decide a time your workday ends, write it down, and stop then, with a short shutdown routine.
**Best for:** Work that expands to fill the evening, and a feeling that you are never finished.
**Skip it when:** You are on call, in a crunch week you have planned for, or your hours are set by others. Use it as a target, not a rule, and be honest about the exceptions. It backfires if you stop and then ruminate about what is unfinished.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Pick a stop time that is realistic on most days, and write it down.
2. Set an alarm 20 minutes earlier labelled "start shutdown".
3. Write a three-item shutdown list: record what is unfinished, write tomorrow's top three, close the work apps.
4. End with a closing sentence you say or write, such as "done for today".
**7-day trial:** Run the shutdown at your stop time each work day. Note each day whether you stopped within 15 minutes of the target. On Day 7, adjust the time or the workload that is pushing past it. Track: days you stopped on time, and what caused overruns.
**Pairs well with:** Daily review; Digital sunset; Anti-goals.
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin.
**Evidence:** Some. Research on detaching from work after hours and on planning unfinished tasks suggests it may ease intrusive thoughts, but studies are small and mixed in design.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Either.
