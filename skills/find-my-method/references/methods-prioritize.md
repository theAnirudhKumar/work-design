# Methods: prioritisation

These twelve methods decide what to do first, what to do later, and what to drop. Some rank a whole list (Ivy Lee, RICE, ABCDE), some sort by a rule (Eisenhower, MoSCoW, Impact/effort matrix), some cap your day (1-3-5, Most Important Tasks), and some guard against taking on the wrong things at all (Hell yeah or no, Not-to-do list). Pick by the shape of your problem: too many items, too many equal-looking items, or too many requests from other people. Evidence labels are conservative; almost all of these are practice with little direct testing, and the cards say so.

## Eisenhower matrix
**One line:** Sort each task into one of four boxes by two yes/no questions, urgent and important: do now, schedule, delegate, or drop.
**Best for:** A list where everything feels urgent, and days spent on other people's emergencies while important work waits.
**Skip it when:** The list is mostly equally urgent and important items that need ranking within a box, or you have no one to delegate to. It also gives no sense of effort.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Write every open task on its own line (aim for 15 to 30).
2. Mark each one Urgent (has a real deadline soon, or someone is waiting) and/or Important (moves a goal that matters to you).
3. Urgent and important: do today. Important only: book a time on the calendar.
4. Urgent only: delegate, shrink, or reply with a short no. Neither: delete.
5. Look at the "important only" box and put a first slot in the calendar this week.
**7-day trial:** Re-sort the list each morning in five minutes. Track the number of items in "important only" that you actually began, and the count you deleted or delegated.
**Pairs well with:** Big rocks; Not-to-do list; Impact/effort matrix.
**Comes from:** Often attributed to Dwight D. Eisenhower, who in a 1954 speech quoted an unnamed college president on urgent versus important problems; the four-box form was popularised later by Stephen Covey, so the attribution to Eisenhower is loose.
**Evidence:** Practice only. No direct testing of the matrix itself; people do tend to favour urgent over important work, which is the problem it aims at.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Either

## Ivy Lee method
**One line:** At the end of each day, write the six most important tasks for tomorrow in order, then work them one at a time from the top, moving unfinished ones to the next day's list.
**Best for:** Decision fatigue about where to start, and people who hop between tasks and finish few.
**Skip it when:** Your day is driven by incoming requests that cannot wait, or tasks differ so much in size that a six-item list holds days of work. Keep the list to what fits the day.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. At the end of today, write the six most important things for tomorrow.
2. Rank them by importance, with 1 as the one that matters most.
3. Next morning, start with item 1 and work on it alone until it is done or you reach a natural stopping point.
4. Then move to item 2. Do not reorder midday unless something truly urgent arrives.
5. At day's end, carry unfinished items over and write the next list.
**7-day trial:** Write the list each evening. Track how many of the six you finished and, separately, whether item 1 was finished every day.
**Pairs well with:** Most Important Tasks; Eat the frog; 1-3-5 rule.
**Comes from:** Often told as consultant Ivy Lee advising an industrialist; the story is a popular anecdote and is not reliably verified.
**Evidence:** Practice only. No direct testing; planning the evening before has some appeal, but no study of this exact method was found.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Solo

## 1-3-5 rule
**One line:** Plan each day with exactly one big task, three medium tasks and five small tasks, nine in total.
**Best for:** People who overload the day with a list that can never be finished, and who need a rough measure of size as well as importance.
**Skip it when:** Your work is one large project with no natural small tasks, or the day is mostly reactive. It differs from Most Important Tasks, which picks by importance only and does not mix sizes.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. List everything you could do today, then circle one big task (a few hours of work).
2. Pick three medium tasks (about an hour each).
3. Pick five small tasks (15 minutes or less), such as replies and calls.
4. Cross out the rest and move them to a "later" list.
5. Order the day: big task first in your best hours, small tasks in the gaps.
**7-day trial:** Use the 1-3-5 shape daily. Track how many of the nine you completed and whether the big task got done; adjust the counts if you finish none or all.
**Pairs well with:** Ivy Lee method; Eat the frog; Big rocks.
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin.
**Evidence:** Practice only. No direct testing; its value is a realistic cap on the day, which is plausible but unproven.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Solo

## Most Important Tasks
**One line:** Each day pick one to three Most Important Tasks (MITs) that would make the day a success if nothing else got done, and do them first.
**Best for:** Busy days that end with plenty done and nothing that mattered, and people who want the lightest possible planning habit.
**Skip it when:** You need to rank a long list, since this only picks the top few, or your top tasks are always interrupted by others. For a mixed-size plan, see the 1-3-5 rule, which adds medium and small tasks.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Ask: "If I did only these today, would the day count?" Write down up to three tasks.
2. Make each one specific and finishable today (not "work on report" but "draft the results section").
3. Write them on a card or sticky note and put it where you will see it first.
4. Do them before email and meetings where you can; at least start the first one.
5. At day's end, tick them or note why not.
**7-day trial:** Choose the MITs the night before. Track how many were done each day and one cause whenever a MIT slipped (interruption, too big, wrong choice).
**Pairs well with:** Eat the frog; Ivy Lee method; Time blocking.
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin; it is widely taught by productivity writers.
**Evidence:** Practice only. No direct testing; related research on specific goals and plans is supportive but is not about this method.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Solo

## Pareto principle
**One line:** Assume a small share of your tasks produces most of your results, find those few, and put your best effort there while trimming the rest.
**Best for:** Overloaded lists where everything looks equally worthy, and work with clear outputs (sales, study topics, features, clients).
**Skip it when:** Results are hard to attribute, you are early in a role and do not know what matters yet, or every item is essential (compliance, safety). The "80/20" ratio is a rough heuristic, not a measured law for your work.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Choose a result you want (for example, income, exam score, a project outcome).
2. List the activities of the past month that fed into it.
3. For each, ask which ones plausibly caused most of the result. Mark the top few as your vital few.
4. Mark the activities that gave little or nothing as candidates to stop, shrink or delegate.
5. Schedule your next week's best hours for the vital few.
**7-day trial:** Spend the first hour of each day on a vital-few item. Track hours on vital-few versus other tasks and whether you cut one low-value task per day.
**Pairs well with:** Not-to-do list; Impact/effort matrix; Big rocks.
**Comes from:** Named for Vilfredo Pareto, an economist who noted an uneven spread of wealth; its application to personal work was added by later writers.
**Evidence:** Practice only. Uneven distribution of results is common, but nobody has tested whether acting on the pattern improves personal productivity.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Either

## ABCDE method
**One line:** Label every task A to E: A must do, B should do, C nice to do, D delegate, E eliminate; then number the A tasks A-1, A-2 and so on, and do A-1 first.
**Best for:** Long lists with no order, and people who put easy C tasks ahead of consequential ones.
**Skip it when:** You have few tasks, or a team that needs shared priorities (the letters are personal, so they will not match another person's). Re-label daily, since tasks change.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Write out the full list for today or this week.
2. Mark each task with a letter: A (serious consequence if undone), B (mild consequence), C (no consequence), D (someone else can do it), E (can be dropped).
3. Number the A tasks in order of importance: A-1, A-2, A-3. Do the same for B tasks.
4. Send D tasks to the right person with a clear request and delete the E tasks.
5. Start A-1, and do not touch a B task until all A tasks are done or blocked.
**7-day trial:** Re-letter every morning. Track how many A tasks were done each day and how many tasks you deleted or delegated.
**Pairs well with:** Eisenhower matrix; Eat the frog; Not-to-do list.
**Comes from:** Popularised by Brian Tracy in his time management writing.
**Evidence:** Practice only. No direct testing; the method is a structured ranking and its worth depends on how honestly you judge "serious consequence".
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Solo

## MoSCoW
**One line:** Sort items into Must have, Should have, Could have and Won't have (this time), so a team agrees what is in scope and what waits.
**Best for:** Projects, events and releases with a fixed deadline and too many requests, and groups who need a shared answer on what is cut first.
**Skip it when:** You are choosing for one person's daily list, or if everything gets labelled Must. The method only works if Musts are limited to what the project fails without.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. List all candidate items or requests (the scope).
2. State the fixed constraint: the date, the budget or the number of people.
3. Mark Must only for items without which the outcome fails or is unsafe or illegal.
4. Mark the rest Should, Could, or Won't (this time), and write one reason for each Won't.
5. Check that the Musts fit within the constraint; if not, move something down or change the constraint openly.
**7-day trial:** Apply it to one live project this week. Track how many items ended up as Must versus the total, and whether stakeholders accepted each Won't without reopening it.
**Pairs well with:** RICE scoring; Impact/effort matrix; Not-to-do list.
**Comes from:** Devised by Dai Clegg at Oracle in 1994 for rapid application development, and later widely used in the DSDM agile project method.
**Evidence:** Practice only. Widely used in project work, with little direct testing of whether it improves outcomes.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Team

## RICE scoring
**One line:** Score each option on Reach, Impact and Confidence, divide by Effort, and rank by the result.
**Best for:** Choosing between many competing projects or features when you have some numbers, and defending a ranking to others.
**Skip it when:** The numbers are guesses and nobody will check them, since the maths makes them look accurate, or the list is short enough to decide by discussion.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. List 5 to 10 options and define a unit for each factor: Reach (people or events in a period), Impact (a 1 to 3 scale), Confidence (a percentage you set honestly), Effort (person-weeks or days).
2. Fill in the four values for each option, writing the source or a note when it is a guess.
3. Compute Reach x Impact x Confidence, divided by Effort.
4. Sort high to low and compare the order with your gut feeling.
5. Investigate any case where they disagree before committing.
**7-day trial:** Score one batch of options and begin the top one. Track how far the actual effort and reach turned out from your estimates, so you can calibrate next time.
**Pairs well with:** MoSCoW; Impact/effort matrix; Pareto principle.
**Comes from:** Popularised by the product team at Intercom, a software company, in a 2018 blog post by Sean McBride.
**Evidence:** Practice only. A structured way to compare options, but no direct testing; the quality of the scores depends on the estimates behind them.
**Effort to set up:** Medium.   **Works for:** Team

## Impact/effort matrix
**One line:** Plot each option on a two-by-two grid of impact (high or low) against effort (high or low) and start with high-impact, low-effort items.
**Best for:** A pile of ideas or improvements where you want quick wins and a clear view of which big bets are worth the effort.
**Skip it when:** Impact cannot be judged without more information, or effort estimates are unreliable (new kinds of work). Also unhelpful for tasks forced on you by others or by rules.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. List the options on sticky notes or lines (up to 20).
2. Draw a cross: impact upward, effort to the right.
3. Place each option by quick judgement, with no more than 30 seconds each.
4. Start with the top-left (high impact, low effort); schedule the top-right as projects; question the bottom-right; do the bottom-left only if cheap.
5. Pick the first two quick wins and set a time to do them.
**7-day trial:** Finish at least one quick win a day. Track how many quick wins you completed and whether any item moved quadrant once you started (effort or impact misjudged).
**Pairs well with:** RICE scoring; Pareto principle; Eisenhower matrix.
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin; it appears in many planning and design toolkits.
**Evidence:** Practice only. No direct testing; it is a fast sorting aid whose results are only as good as your judgement of impact and effort.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Either

## Big rocks
**One line:** Schedule your few largest priorities first (the big rocks) so that the small tasks fill the gaps, rather than the reverse.
**Best for:** Weeks full of small jobs where the large, important work (a thesis chapter, a relationship, a health habit) never gets a slot.
**Skip it when:** Your schedule is set by others and has no free hours, or you have not decided what your big rocks are. A priority that is not on the calendar is only a wish.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Write down your two or three big rocks for this week, across work and life.
2. For each, estimate the hours it needs and when it is best done.
3. Put each into the calendar as a block before anything else.
4. Lay out fixed commitments and small tasks around them.
5. Defend the blocks: when something new arrives, ask what moves out to make room.
**7-day trial:** Place the rocks on Sunday or Monday. Track the hours each rock actually received compared with the plan, and one thing you declined or moved to protect them.
**Pairs well with:** Eisenhower matrix; Ideal week; 1-3-5 rule.
**Comes from:** Popularised by Stephen Covey, who told it in First Things First as a demonstration with a jar, rocks and sand; he presented it as a story he had heard, not his own invention.
**Evidence:** Practice only. The jar demonstration is an illustration, not a study; the underlying planning habit of committing time ahead is plausible but untested as a method.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Either

## Hell yeah or no
**One line:** When asked to take on something new, accept only if your reaction is "hell yeah, I want to do that", and otherwise say no.
**Best for:** A calendar full of "sure, why not" commitments, and people who say yes to avoid awkwardness.
**Skip it when:** The request is part of your job or duty (a shift, a parent's care), when you are new and need to say yes to learn, or when you would use it to avoid every hard thing. It filters optional commitments only.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. List the commitments you accepted in the past month that you did not really want.
2. For the next request, pause and check your gut: does it feel like a clear yes?
3. If it is lukewarm, write a short decline (for example, "Thanks for asking, I can't take this on right now").
4. Send it within a day rather than leaving it hanging.
5. Review one current commitment and consider stepping out of it.
**7-day trial:** Apply the test to every optional request. Track the number of requests you received, how many you declined, and how many hours that freed.
**Pairs well with:** Not-to-do list; Big rocks; Eisenhower matrix.
**Comes from:** Derek Sivers, who wrote about this rule for deciding what to say yes to.
**Evidence:** Practice only. No direct testing; it is a decision rule whose benefit depends on your situation.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Solo

## Not-to-do list
**One line:** Keep a written list of things you have decided to stop doing, so that your time is freed by subtraction as well as by planning.
**Best for:** People who already work hard and have no spare time to add another system, and recurring low-value habits that eat hours.
**Skip it when:** The items on it are required by others, or you treat it as a guilt list. It works for choices you control; for others' requests use Hell yeah or no.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Review last week's calendar and notes for recurring time sinks.
2. Pick 3 to 5 that give little back (a standing meeting you rarely need, checking messages in bed, a report nobody reads).
3. Write each as a clear rule: "I will not ...".
4. For each, write what you will do instead, or simply do nothing.
5. Put the list where you will see it, and tell anyone affected.
**7-day trial:** Follow the list for the week. Track the number of times you broke each rule and the hours you estimate you recovered; keep, change or drop each rule at day 7.
**Pairs well with:** Hell yeah or no; Pareto principle; Eisenhower matrix.
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin.
**Evidence:** Practice only. No direct testing; stopping low-value activities is plausible, but the list format has not been studied.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Either
