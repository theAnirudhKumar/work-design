# Methods: time and schedule

These twelve methods decide when work happens and how long a stretch of it lasts: fixed work-and-rest cycles, blocks on a calendar, deadlines, and matching hard work to the hours when you are sharpest. They fix different frictions, so read "Best for" before "One line". Evidence labels are conservative. Most of these are widely used practice with little direct testing, and where a related finding exists the card says so without citing numbers. Health-adjacent advice here (breaks, rest, daily rhythm) is general wellbeing practice, not medical advice; follow a professional's advice for any condition.

## Pomodoro Technique
**One line:** Work on one task for 25 minutes with a timer, take a 5-minute break, and repeat; after four rounds take a longer break of 15 to 30 minutes.
**Best for:** Starting dreaded or vague tasks, and people who drift into email or tabs after a few minutes.
**Skip it when:** You are in deep flow (a timer cuts it off), your work is interrupted by other people all day, or you are doing collaborative work that cannot pause every 25 minutes.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Pick one task and write what "done for this round" looks like in one line.
2. Put your phone out of reach and close the tabs you do not need.
3. Set a timer for 25 minutes and work on that task only.
4. When it rings, stand up for 5 minutes away from the screen. Do not check messages.
5. Make a tick mark for the round, then start the next one.
**7-day trial:** Do 4 rounds a day, on your hardest task first. Track rounds completed per day and how many times you broke the rule mid-round (checked a phone, switched task).
**Pairs well with:** Task batching; Time blocking; Eat the frog.
**Comes from:** Francesco Cirillo, who developed it in the late 1980s using a tomato-shaped kitchen timer.
**Evidence:** Some. Research on regular breaks supports the idea that short rests help sustain attention, but the specific 25/5 split has not been shown to beat other splits.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Solo

## Flowtime
**One line:** Work until your focus naturally fades, note the start and stop times, then take a break proportional to how long you worked.
**Best for:** People who find a fixed 25-minute timer breaks their concentration just as it gets going.
**Skip it when:** You tend to avoid starting or to quit early with no rule to stop you; the open-ended length gives a procrastinator no structure.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Choose one task and write the time you start on a sticky note.
2. Work with no timer running. When you notice your attention slipping for good, stop.
3. Write the stop time and the minutes worked.
4. Take a break of roughly one-fifth to one-third of that time (about 10 minutes after an hour of work), away from screens.
5. Start the next stretch the same way.
**7-day trial:** Log every stretch (start, stop, minutes, task) on paper or in a note. At day 7, look at the average and longest stretch and at which hours gave the longest ones.
**Pairs well with:** Prime-time scheduling; Time blocking; 90-minute focus blocks.
**Comes from:** Often attributed to Zoe Read-Bivens as a flexible alternative to Pomodoro; the attribution rests mostly on secondary sources and the date is unclear.
**Evidence:** Practice only. No direct testing found; it rests on the general idea that attention varies by person and task.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Solo

## 52/17 rule
**One line:** Work with full focus for 52 minutes, then rest for 17 minutes away from your desk, and repeat.
**Best for:** Desk work that needs sustained concentration, where 25 minutes feels too short and a full 90 feels too long.
**Skip it when:** Your day is chopped by meetings and calls, so 52 clear minutes rarely exist, or you use the 17 minutes to scroll, which is not rest.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Pick the one task for the next block and write its finish line.
2. Silence notifications and set a timer for 52 minutes.
3. Work on that task only. Park stray thoughts on a list rather than acting on them.
4. At the timer, leave the desk for 17 minutes: walk, stretch, water, daylight.
5. Return and decide before sitting down what the next block is for.
**7-day trial:** Do three blocks a day on days you have the time. Track blocks completed and a 1 to 5 rating of how rested the 17-minute break left you; compare to your usual day.
**Pairs well with:** Time blocking; Prime-time scheduling; Day theming.
**Comes from:** Popularised by an analysis from DeskTime, a time-tracking app, of its own most productive users; it was observational, not a controlled test, and a later DeskTime update reported different figures, so treat the numbers as uncertain.
**Evidence:** Practice only. Break research is supportive in general, but nothing shows 52/17 beats other work-rest ratios; treat the numbers as a starting point.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Solo

## 90-minute focus blocks
**One line:** Protect one or two uninterrupted blocks of about 90 minutes for your hardest work, with a real break of 15 to 20 minutes after each.
**Best for:** Writing, coding, studying, design, analysis, or anything with a long warm-up where interruptions are costly.
**Skip it when:** Your role is reactive (nursing shifts, support queues, on-call), or you cannot guard 90 minutes, since a block broken at minute 20 is worse than a shorter planned one.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Find the single best 90-minute slot tomorrow and block it on your calendar as busy.
2. Name the one task for it and a clear outcome (for example, "draft sections 1 and 2").
3. Decide what you will switch off: notifications, door, phone in another room.
4. During the block, keep a distraction list instead of acting on urges.
5. After it, take a proper break: move, eat, no feeds.
**7-day trial:** Hold one block a day, two if the week allows. Track blocks that ran uninterrupted for at least 75 minutes and what you produced in each.
**Pairs well with:** Prime-time scheduling; Time blocking; Day theming.
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin. Often linked to the idea of body rhythms of roughly 90 minutes, a claim that is contested.
**Evidence:** Practice only. The idea that people have a fixed 90-minute focus cycle is contested, though a long protected block is plausibly useful for complex work.
**Effort to set up:** Medium.   **Works for:** Either

## Time blocking
**One line:** Assign every part of your workday to a specific block on your calendar, including tasks, admin, meetings and breaks, then follow the calendar instead of a to-do list.
**Best for:** Days that disappear without a trace, and people who have a long list but never decide when anything will happen.
**Skip it when:** Your day is mostly unpredictable (support, emergency work, caregiving), or you will treat a blown schedule as failure and abandon the practice. Compare Timeboxing, which limits how long a task may take rather than when it sits in the day.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. List tomorrow's tasks with a rough time estimate for each.
2. Add fixed commitments first (meetings, school run, shift start).
3. Place the most important task in your best-energy slot, then the rest around it.
4. Add a 15-minute buffer between blocks and one block for email and messages.
5. Mark a replan point mid-afternoon to shuffle what slipped.
**7-day trial:** Block each day the evening before. Track the share of blocks you started roughly on time and your honest estimate error (planned minutes versus actual) for the top task.
**Pairs well with:** Ideal week; Task batching; Pomodoro Technique.
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin; its use is widespread and the history is unclear.
**Evidence:** Some. Studies on planning exactly when and where you will act support the idea behind it, but time blocking as a whole has had little direct testing.
**Effort to set up:** Medium.   **Works for:** Either

## Timeboxing
**One line:** Give a task a fixed amount of time up front, and when the time is up you stop and review rather than keep going until it feels finished.
**Best for:** Perfectionism, tasks that expand to fill all available time, and open-ended jobs such as research or tidying.
**Skip it when:** The work has a hard quality bar that time cannot satisfy (safety checks, clinical documentation), or the box is so short you rush. It differs from Time blocking: a time box caps duration and forces a decision at the end, while a block just reserves a place on the calendar.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Pick one task that tends to sprawl, such as an email batch or a first draft.
2. Decide the box using your last similar effort, then shorten it a little (for example, 40 minutes).
3. Write what a "good enough" result looks like at the end of the box.
4. Set a timer and stop when it rings, even if unfinished.
5. Decide at the stop: ship it, extend once by a set amount, or reschedule the remainder.
**7-day trial:** Box one sprawling task a day. Track whether you shipped something at the end of each box and how much of the extra time you would have used was genuinely needed.
**Pairs well with:** Parkinson's-law deadlines; Pomodoro Technique; Time blocking.
**Comes from:** The software "timebox" is described in James Martin's 1991 book Rapid Application Development and was later central to agile iterations; the broader personal use is folk practice.
**Evidence:** Practice only. Related research on deadlines and goals suggests limits can focus effort, but timeboxing itself has been little tested.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Either

## Task batching
**One line:** Group similar small tasks (email, calls, invoices, errands) and do them together in one session instead of scattered through the day.
**Best for:** Frequent switching between shallow tasks, and the feeling of being busy all day without finishing anything.
**Skip it when:** A batch delays something urgent others are waiting on, or the work needs a fast response (inbox for customers, patient messages). Large creative tasks do not batch well either.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Look at the last two days and list the small recurring tasks you did in scattered moments.
2. Sort them into two or three categories: messages, admin, errands, calls.
3. Choose a fixed slot for each category (for example, messages at 11:00 and 16:00).
4. Tell the people who depend on you when you will answer, if it is a change.
5. Keep a "next batch" note where stray items go until their slot.
**7-day trial:** Batch messages and one other category each day. Track the number of times you checked messages outside the slots and how many tasks you completed in each batch.
**Pairs well with:** Time blocking; Day theming; Pomodoro Technique.
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin; the idea is old in manufacturing and was adopted into personal productivity.
**Evidence:** Some. Research shows that switching between tasks costs time and accuracy, which supports the logic, but batching itself has had little direct testing.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Either

## Day theming
**One line:** Give each weekday a main focus (for example, Monday for planning, Tuesday for deep writing, Friday for admin) so that most days carry one type of work.
**Best for:** People who juggle several roles or projects and lose time to constant context switching between them.
**Skip it when:** Your schedule is set by others (shifts, rotating clinics, classes), or you have an urgent stream that ignores your themes. Keep the themes soft, not rules.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. List your three to five recurring areas of work or life.
2. Look at your fixed meetings and see which days are naturally heavy.
3. Assign one theme per weekday, keeping meeting-heavy days for meeting work.
4. Write the plan on a card or in the calendar header.
5. Leave one half day unthemed as overflow.
**7-day trial:** Follow the themes for a full week. Track the number of days you spent mostly on the theme and one thing you completed under each.
**Pairs well with:** Ideal week; Task batching; 90-minute focus blocks.
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin. It is sometimes linked to well-known founders who run several companies, but the attribution is loose.
**Evidence:** Practice only. No direct testing; it rests on the general finding that switching between kinds of work costs time.
**Effort to set up:** Medium.   **Works for:** Either

## Ideal week
**One line:** Design a template week once, with recurring blocks for focus, meetings, admin, exercise and family, then plan each real week by adjusting that template.
**Best for:** Weeks that feel reactive, and people who want work, rest and life areas all visible and protected in one place.
**Skip it when:** Your hours change week to week and a template never matches, or you will spend the whole weekend perfecting it. Build a rough version first.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Draw or open an empty 7-day grid from wake-up to bedtime.
2. Fill in fixed items: work hours, commute, school, sleep, standing meetings.
3. Place two or three focus blocks in your best-energy hours.
4. Add the non-work items you want protected: exercise, a meal with others, time alone.
5. Save it as a copy and use it to plan next week.
**7-day trial:** Live the template for one week. Each night mark every block as kept, moved or lost. At day 7, tally which blocks were lost most and why, then adjust the template.
**Pairs well with:** Time blocking; Day theming; Prime-time scheduling.
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin; several productivity writers have promoted the idea.
**Evidence:** Practice only. No direct testing; it is a planning habit that bundles time blocking with deliberate life balance.
**Effort to set up:** Medium.   **Works for:** Either

## Eat the frog
**One line:** Do your single most dreaded or most important task first thing, before email or anything easier.
**Best for:** Chronic avoidance of one big task, and mornings that fill up with trivial work until energy is gone.
**Skip it when:** Your first hour is needed for something time-critical (a handover, a school run, an urgent customer), or the dreaded task needs input you do not have yet.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Tonight, write the one task you most want to avoid or that matters most tomorrow.
2. Write its first physical action in one sentence (for example, "open the file and write the heading").
3. Prepare what you need: file open, materials on the desk.
4. Tomorrow, start it before opening email or messages and work at least 25 minutes.
5. Afterward, note whether it was as bad as expected.
**7-day trial:** Pick one frog each day and do it first. Track whether it started within 30 minutes of beginning work and whether you finished it or reached the set stopping point.
**Pairs well with:** Pomodoro Technique; Most Important Tasks; Prime-time scheduling.
**Comes from:** Popularised by Brian Tracy's book Eat That Frog!. The quote is often attributed to Mark Twain, but Quote Investigator found no evidence that he said it.
**Evidence:** Practice only. No direct testing; it assumes willpower and energy are highest early, which is not true for everyone.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Solo

## Prime-time scheduling
**One line:** Work out when your energy and focus peak and place your hardest work there, leaving low-energy hours for routine tasks.
**Best for:** People who do the same quality of work across the day and burn their sharpest hours on email, meetings or admin.
**Skip it when:** Your hours are set by shifts or others' calendars and you cannot move anything, or your peak does not exist as a pattern. Start by noticing, not by rearranging.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Recall the last week and note when you felt sharpest and when you were slow.
2. Rate your energy 1 to 5 at three points in tomorrow (for example, 9:00, 13:00, 16:00).
3. Choose the best two-hour window as your prime time and mark it on the calendar.
4. Move one hard task into it. Keep meetings and email out of it where you can.
5. Put routine work (admin, forms, errands) into your low-energy window.
**7-day trial:** Log energy 1 to 5 three times a day for the week, plus what you worked on in your best window. At day 7, pick the window that held up and protect it next week.
**Pairs well with:** Eat the frog; 90-minute focus blocks; Ideal week.
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin.
**Evidence:** Some. Research on time-of-day effects and individual body-clock differences supports the basic idea, but patterns vary a lot between people and the gains for any one person are not predictable.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Solo

## Parkinson's-law deadlines
**One line:** Set a shorter deadline on purpose so the work stops expanding to fill the time you gave it.
**Best for:** Tasks that always take the whole afternoon, polishing past the point of use, and open-ended deadlines.
**Skip it when:** Quality or safety matters more than speed, the work depends on others' replies, or a tighter limit would make you anxious and sloppy. Compare Timeboxing, which caps a single sitting; this method shortens the overall deadline.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Choose one task with a loose deadline.
2. Estimate how long the real work takes with no interruptions, then shave that by about a quarter.
3. Set a deadline for it, and tell someone or book a hand-off to make it real.
4. Remove optional extras: write a list of what is not in scope.
5. When the deadline arrives, ship or hand off what you have and note what you cut.
**7-day trial:** Shorten one deadline per day (pick small tasks). Track whether you finished on the shorter deadline and rate the result as acceptable, better or worse than usual.
**Pairs well with:** Timeboxing; Time blocking; Eat the frog.
**Comes from:** Named for C. Northcote Parkinson, who stated "work expands so as to fill the time available" as a humorous observation about organisations; its use as a self-management tactic is folk practice.
**Evidence:** Mixed. Tighter deadlines can focus effort, but they can also lower quality and raise stress, and effects depend heavily on the person and the task.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Either
