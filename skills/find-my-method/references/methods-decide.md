# Methods: decisions

These methods help when a decision is stuck, repeated or too heavy: what job to take, whether to buy, whom to hire, what to do next. They do not make the decision for you. They make the trade-offs visible, cap the time spent, or check your thinking from another angle. Match the method to the stakes: small, reversible choices need speed, large, one-way choices need care. Decisions about health, money or legal matters may need a qualified professional.

## 10-10-10 rule
**One line:** Ask how you will feel about each option in 10 minutes, 10 months and 10 years.
**Best for:** Choices that feel urgent but are shaped by short-term emotion (sending an angry email, a purchase, a hard conversation).
**Skip it when:** The decision depends on facts you have not gathered, or the future feelings are too uncertain to guess.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Write the decision and its two or three options.
2. For each option, write how you expect to feel and what will likely have happened at 10 minutes, 10 months and 10 years.
3. Mark where the options differ the most across the three time scales.
4. Note any option that is good short-term and bad long-term, or the reverse.
5. Choose, or name the one fact you still need.
**7-day trial:** Use it on one real decision per day, small or medium, for at least three days. Track: whether the answer changed your first instinct, and later, whether you were glad of the choice.
**Pairs well with:** Regret minimization, Sleep-on-it rule, Weighted decision matrix
**Comes from:** Suzy Welch, who described it in her writing and in a 2009 book on the method.
**Evidence:** Practice only. Intuitively useful and widely quoted, with little direct testing.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Solo

## Regret minimization
**One line:** Imagine yourself at 80 and ask which choice you would regret more, taking it or not taking it.
**Best for:** Big life choices where fear holds you back (a career change, starting something, moving).
**Skip it when:** The choice is mostly about numbers or logistics, or fear is actually warning you of a real risk you have not examined.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Write the decision and the option you are drawn to but afraid of.
2. Picture yourself at 80 looking back. Write what you would say about trying, and about not trying.
3. Separate regret from failure: a failed attempt may be less regretted than never trying.
4. List what would make the leap safer (savings, a trial period, an exit plan).
5. Decide, or set a date for deciding.
**7-day trial:** Spend 10 minutes a day for a week on one big decision, repeating the 80-year-old view and adding to the safer-leap list. Track: how your answer shifts across the week, and the first action you would take.
**Pairs well with:** 10-10-10 rule, Two-way door decisions, Inversion
**Comes from:** Jeff Bezos described using this framework for his decision to start Amazon.
**Evidence:** Practice only. Research on regret shows people often regret inaction more over long periods, but the framework itself has not been tested as a decision method.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Solo

## Weighted decision matrix
**One line:** Score each option against criteria you have weighted by importance, then compare totals.
**Best for:** Choosing between three to six options on several criteria (job offers, tools, vendors, flats).
**Skip it when:** The decision is simple or emotional, or you would adjust the weights until your favourite wins; then use the result as a conversation, not a verdict.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. List the options in rows and five to seven criteria in columns.
2. Give each criterion a weight from 1 to 5 for how much it matters.
3. Score each option on each criterion from 1 to 5.
4. Multiply score by weight and total each row.
5. Check the result against your gut. If they disagree, find out which criterion you left out.
**7-day trial:** Build the matrix on day 1, then wait. On days 2 to 6 check the weights once a day and change them only with a reason. On day 7 decide. Track: whether the matrix and your gut agree, and what the disagreement revealed.
**Pairs well with:** Decision journal, Sleep-on-it rule, Satisficing
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin; it is a standard tool in decision analysis, and Benjamin Franklin described a similar pros-and-cons approach.
**Evidence:** Some. Structured decision aids tend to make choices more consistent, though they cannot fix poor criteria or invented scores.
**Effort to set up:** Medium.   **Works for:** Either

## Decision journal
**One line:** Write down each important decision, your reasoning and expectations at the time, then review the outcome later.
**Best for:** Learning from your choices, and separating a bad decision from bad luck.
**Skip it when:** You have few significant decisions, or you would write it and never look back, which makes it just a diary.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Create a note or notebook with these fields: date, decision, options, what I expect to happen, confidence (0 to 100), mood, review date.
2. Log one pending decision now.
3. Write your reasons before the outcome, not after.
4. Set a review date in your calendar.
5. At the review, compare the prediction to what happened and note what you would do differently.
**7-day trial:** Log every decision of any weight (even small) for seven days, aiming for three or more entries. Track: number of entries, and on day 7 pick one older small decision to review. Keep the log going if it teaches you something.
**Pairs well with:** Weighted decision matrix, Inversion, Sleep-on-it rule
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin.
**Evidence:** Practice only. Favoured by investors and managers and plausible as a feedback tool, with little direct testing.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Solo

## Sleep-on-it rule
**One line:** For decisions that are not urgent, wait a night before committing.
**Best for:** Impulse buys, emotional replies, and offers that arrive with pressure.
**Skip it when:** A deadline is real and short, or waiting becomes avoidance and the decision just drifts. Set a decide-by time when you start the wait.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Write the decision and the time you will decide by (tomorrow, 10am).
2. Write down your current leaning and why.
3. List the one or two facts that would change your mind.
4. Stop thinking about it and do something else.
5. Next day, read your note first, then decide or name what is still missing.
**7-day trial:** Apply the rule to every non-urgent purchase or reply over a set value or weight that you pick yourself. Track: how many times your answer changed overnight, and how many "must have it now" wants faded.
**Pairs well with:** 10-10-10 rule, Decision journal, Two-way door decisions
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin.
**Evidence:** Mixed. Research on whether sleep or a break improves decisions gives inconsistent results, but a pause plainly helps cool impulses. Sleep needs are personal; follow a professional's advice for any sleep condition.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Either

## Two-way door decisions
**One line:** Sort decisions into reversible (two-way door) and hard to reverse (one-way door), and decide fast on the first kind.
**Best for:** Overthinking small or reversible choices, and slow teams that treat every decision as high stakes.
**Skip it when:** You misclassify a one-way decision as reversible to avoid the work, or the cost of reversing is high but hidden.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. List the decisions waiting on you.
2. For each, ask: if this turns out wrong, how hard, costly and slow is it to undo?
3. Mark each as a two-way door (easy to undo) or a one-way door (hard).
4. Decide the two-way ones today with the information you have, and set a date to review them.
5. For one-way doors, set time to gather evidence and consult people.
**7-day trial:** Label every decision this week. Track: how many two-way doors you decided within a day, and whether any you decided quickly needed reversing, and how costly that was.
**Pairs well with:** Satisficing, Regret minimization, Decision journal
**Comes from:** Jeff Bezos, in Amazon shareholder letters, where it appears as reversible versus irreversible decisions.
**Evidence:** Practice only. Used widely in business, with little direct testing.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Either

## Inversion
**One line:** Instead of asking how to succeed, ask how you would fail or what to avoid, then do the opposite.
**Best for:** Plans and goals where you cannot see the risks, and problems where the forward approach is stuck.
**Skip it when:** You are already anxious and the exercise feeds worry without producing actions, or the decision needs options, not risks.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Write the goal or decision.
2. Ask: what would guarantee failure or regret here? List 8 to 10 answers.
3. Mark the ones you are currently doing or risking.
4. Turn each marked item into an avoid rule or a safeguard.
5. Keep the top three rules where you can see them.
**7-day trial:** Invert one decision or goal per day, with a different one each day. Track: the number of avoid rules you made, and the one you acted on that day.
**Pairs well with:** Decision journal, Regret minimization, Weighted decision matrix
**Comes from:** Charlie Munger popularised it, citing the mathematician Carl Jacobi's advice to invert.
**Evidence:** Practice only. Related to premortem-style thinking, but this specific method has not been tested directly.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Either

## Satisficing
**One line:** Decide what is good enough before you look, then pick the first option that meets it instead of hunting for the best.
**Best for:** Choice overload and endless comparing on low and medium stakes decisions (what to buy, where to eat, which tool).
**Skip it when:** The stakes are high and the options differ a lot, or the cost of a poor pick is large and permanent.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Write the decision.
2. List the three to five must-haves. Anything else is a bonus.
3. Set a limit on the search: a time (20 minutes) or a number of options (three).
4. Look at options until one meets all the must-haves.
5. Choose it and stop looking. Write one line on what you gained by stopping.
**7-day trial:** Use it on a small decision each day. Track: time spent per decision, and a 1-to-5 satisfaction rating a day later. Compare with a recent decision you maximised.
**Pairs well with:** Two-way door decisions, Weighted decision matrix, Sleep-on-it rule
**Comes from:** Herbert Simon, who introduced the term in the 1950s.
**Evidence:** Mixed. Studies comparing people who seek the best with those who accept good enough tend to link satisficing with more contentment, but results vary and are not all about outcomes.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Solo
