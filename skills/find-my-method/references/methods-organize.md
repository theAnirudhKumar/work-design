# Methods: capture and organise

These twelve methods answer one question: where does a task, idea or commitment go the moment it appears, and how do you find it again? Use them when things live in your head, in five apps or on scraps of paper. Pick one method to try for a week, not several. Evidence labels are conservative: most organising systems are widely used and rarely tested directly, and the cards say so.

## Getting Things Done
**One line:** Empty your head into one inbox, decide the next physical action for each item, and review everything weekly so you trust the lists.
**Best for:** Overwhelm from many open loops across work and home, and the feeling you are forgetting something.
**Skip it when:** You have few commitments, or you will not do the weekly review; the lists rot without it and you stop trusting them.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Set a 10-minute timer and write every open item on paper, one per line, without sorting.
2. For each line, ask "what is the very next physical action?" and rewrite it as a verb phrase ("Call the school office about the form").
3. Split the lines into: do now if under 2 minutes, a Next Actions list, a Waiting-for list, a Someday/maybe list.
4. Put one weekly 30-minute review in your calendar.
**7-day trial:** Capture into one inbox all week and clear it once a day into the four lists. Track: items found in your head after the dump (should fall), and whether the weekly review happened.
**Pairs well with:** Brain dump; Waiting-for list; Someday/maybe list
**Comes from:** David Allen, in his book Getting Things Done.
**Evidence:** Practice only. The full system has little direct testing; there is indirect support for writing unfinished tasks down so they stop nagging at you.
**Effort to set up:** Medium.   **Works for:** Solo

## PARA
**One line:** Sort everything you keep, notes and files alike, into four folders by how actionable it is: Projects, Areas, Resources, Archive.
**Best for:** Notes and files scattered across apps with no logic for where anything goes.
**Skip it when:** Your real problem is task overload rather than information clutter, or you spend more time reorganising folders than using what is in them.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Create four top-level folders (in your notes app or file storage): Projects, Areas, Resources, Archive.
2. List your active projects (finite, with an end) and make one subfolder for each.
3. List ongoing responsibilities with no end date (health, finances, a team you manage) as Areas.
4. Drag the 10 most recent loose notes or files into the best-fitting folder; anything unclear goes to Resources.
**7-day trial:** File every new note straight into one of the four. On day 7, move finished projects to Archive. Track: how many seconds it takes to find a note you need, and how many items you could not place.
**Pairs well with:** Zettelkasten; Brain dump; Getting Things Done
**Comes from:** Tiago Forte.
**Evidence:** Practice only. It is a filing convention with no direct testing.
**Effort to set up:** Medium.   **Works for:** Solo

## Zettelkasten
**One line:** Write small notes, one idea each, in your own words, and link each to related notes so ideas build on one another.
**Best for:** Students, researchers and writers who read a lot and want to turn reading into their own thinking and writing.
**Skip it when:** You mainly need to run tasks and deadlines; it is a thinking and writing tool, not a to-do system, and it can become note-collecting for its own sake.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Pick one thing you read recently and write one idea from it as a single note, in full sentences, in your own words.
2. Give the note a title that states the idea.
3. Find one older note it relates to and write a link and one sentence on why.
4. Add a short list of source details at the bottom.
**7-day trial:** Write 2 to 3 linked notes a day from what you read. Track: notes written, and whether on day 7 you can draft a paragraph from the notes without reopening the sources.
**Pairs well with:** PARA; Brain dump
**Comes from:** Associated with the sociologist Niklas Luhmann and his paper slip-box; the digital note-linking version is a later adaptation by others.
**Evidence:** Practice only. Writing ideas in your own words has general learning support, but this specific system has not been tested directly.
**Effort to set up:** Medium.   **Works for:** Solo

## Inbox zero
**One line:** Treat your inbox as a place to process mail, not store it: every message gets one decision and leaves.
**Best for:** An inbox with hundreds of unread messages where important ones get lost.
**Skip it when:** You check email constantly already; chasing zero can become a compulsive loop and a way to avoid real work.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Create three folders or labels: Action, Waiting, Reference. Everything else is archived.
2. Archive or bulk-select everything older than two weeks into an "Old" folder, and decide you will search it, not read it.
3. Take the remaining messages one at a time and apply one of: delete, reply now if under 2 minutes, move to Action, move to Waiting, archive.
**7-day trial:** Process the inbox to empty twice a day at fixed times, not continuously. Track: messages left in the inbox at day end, and the number of times you opened email outside the two slots.
**Pairs well with:** Email batching; Waiting-for list; Notification batching
**Comes from:** Merlin Mann, who coined the phrase and wrote about the approach.
**Evidence:** Practice only. Studies of email habits are mixed and none test this approach directly.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Solo

## Bullet Journal
**One line:** A paper notebook with a daily list, a monthly view and short symbols that mark tasks, events and notes, with unfinished items moved forward on purpose.
**Best for:** People who think better on paper, want one place for tasks, notes and a calendar, and like a short daily writing habit.
**Skip it when:** You need reminders and alerts, need to share lists, or will turn the notebook into an art project that costs more time than it saves.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Take any notebook and number the first four pages as an index.
2. Write today's date at the top of a page and list tasks with a dot, events with a circle, notes with a dash.
3. Mark each task done with an X when finished.
4. Tomorrow, before writing new items, look at yesterday's open dots and decide for each: do it today, move it forward, or cross it out.
**7-day trial:** Use only the daily list and the evening migrate step. Track: how many tasks you crossed out on purpose rather than carried forever, and whether you opened the notebook each morning.
**Pairs well with:** Brain dump; Autofocus; Personal Kanban
**Comes from:** Ryder Carroll.
**Evidence:** Practice only. Paper journaling has related research on writing, but this system is not directly tested.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Solo

## Personal Kanban
**One line:** Put your work on a board with columns like To do, Doing and Done, and cap how many items can be in Doing at once.
**Best for:** Starting many things and finishing few, or not being able to see all your work at a glance.
**Skip it when:** Your work is mostly fixed appointments and time-boxed tasks, where a calendar fits better than a board.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Draw three columns on paper, a whiteboard or a free board app: To do, Doing, Done.
2. Write each open task on its own sticky note or card and put them in To do.
3. Set a work-in-progress limit for Doing (2 or 3 is common) and write it above the column.
4. Move your top items into Doing up to the limit; start only from Doing.
**7-day trial:** Pull a new card into Doing only when one moves to Done. Track: how many cards you finished, and how many days a card sat in Doing.
**Pairs well with:** Monotasking; Getting Things Done; Personal Scrum
**Comes from:** Jim Benson, adapting Kanban boards from manufacturing.
**Evidence:** Practice only. Limiting work in progress is common in team settings, but personal use is not directly tested.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Either

## Personal Scrum
**One line:** Plan work in short fixed cycles, usually one week: pick a small set of tasks, do them, then look back on what worked.
**Best for:** Projects that drift, goals with no rhythm, and people who like a regular chance to adjust.
**Skip it when:** Your work is unpredictable day to day (on-call, support, shift swaps), where a weekly commitment is broken constantly.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Write a backlog: every task you might do, in rough priority order.
2. Choose this week's sprint: only as many items as you honestly finished last week, or fewer if unsure.
3. Write the sprint goal in one sentence.
4. Put a 20-minute "look back" on your calendar for the end of the week.
**7-day trial:** Work only from the sprint list; new requests go to the backlog. On the last day, write one thing to keep, one to change. Track: sprint items finished out of planned.
**Pairs well with:** Personal Kanban; Getting Things Done
**Comes from:** Scrum comes from software development, associated with Jeff Sutherland and Ken Schwaber; the personal adaptation has no single origin.
**Evidence:** Practice only. Team Scrum has been studied with mixed results; the personal version has not been tested.
**Effort to set up:** Medium.   **Works for:** Either

## Zen to Done
**One line:** A simplified relative of Getting Things Done that has you build ten habits one at a time, such as capture, process, plan and do.
**Best for:** People who found GTD too heavy and want to add one small habit per month.
**Skip it when:** You already have a working system, or you try to adopt all ten habits at once.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Pick one habit only: collect everything into a single inbox.
2. Choose that inbox (one notebook or one app) and write its name at the top of a page.
3. Put a daily reminder at a fixed time to empty it into a list.
**7-day trial:** Practise only that one habit all week. Track: days you followed it, and the number of captures you made. Add a second habit only next week if the first held.
**Pairs well with:** Getting Things Done; Brain dump; Shutdown ritual
**Comes from:** Leo Babauta.
**Evidence:** Practice only. The habit-by-habit approach is untested; building one habit at a time has general support from habit research.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Solo

## Autofocus
**One line:** Keep one running list, scan it in order, and do whatever stands out; items you do not touch stay, items you work on get re-entered at the end.
**Best for:** A long, stale list you avoid, and people who dislike planning and prefer to follow instinct.
**Skip it when:** You have hard deadlines that must not be missed, because the method does not surface them by itself.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Write all tasks on one page in a notebook, one per line, in the order they came to you.
2. Read the page from top to bottom without acting.
3. Go back through and mark the first item that "stands out" for you now; work on it for as long as you feel like.
4. When you stop, cross it out; if it is not finished, write it again at the end of the list.
**7-day trial:** Work only this way for the week, with deadline items in a separate calendar. Track: tasks you completed and how many old items you finally closed.
**Pairs well with:** Brain dump; Bullet Journal
**Comes from:** Mark Forster.
**Evidence:** Practice only. It is described by its author and users, with no direct testing.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Solo

## Brain dump
**One line:** Write down everything on your mind, unsorted, in one sitting.
**Best for:** A head full of tasks at 11pm, racing thoughts about things to do, or a fresh start on a messy week.
**Skip it when:** You do the dump and never sort it; the list becomes another pile.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Take a blank page or note and set a 7-minute timer.
2. Write whatever comes up, work, home, worries, ideas, one per line, with no order or editing.
3. When the timer ends, circle anything due in the next 48 hours.
4. Copy those into tomorrow's list, and leave the rest on the page.
**7-day trial:** Do a dump each evening for 7 minutes. Track: how many items you circled, and how you rate your mind on a 1 to 5 scale before bed.
**Pairs well with:** Getting Things Done; Shutdown ritual; Someday/maybe list
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin.
**Evidence:** Some. Studies on writing down unfinished tasks or worries suggest it can ease intrusive thoughts, though the settings differ and effects vary by person.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Solo

## Someday/maybe list
**One line:** A parking list for things you might do one day, kept out of your active tasks and checked on a schedule.
**Best for:** Good ideas and wishes that clutter your task list and make it feel endless.
**Skip it when:** You use it as a graveyard and never look at it, or it is full of obligations that really need a decision.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Create one list titled "Someday/maybe" in your notebook or app.
2. Go through your task list and move anything with no date and no commitment into it.
3. Add 5 ideas from your head (a course, a trip, a side project).
4. Set a recurring reminder to read the list once a month.
**7-day trial:** For a week, send every new "that would be nice" to this list instead of the task list. Track: the number of items moved off your active list, and your active list's length on day 7.
**Pairs well with:** Getting Things Done; Brain dump
**Comes from:** Part of Getting Things Done (David Allen).
**Evidence:** Practice only. It is a filing habit with no direct testing.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Solo

## Waiting-for list
**One line:** A list of everything you have handed to someone else or are waiting on, with the date and the person.
**Best for:** Tasks that stall because you forgot you were waiting, and delegating without losing track.
**Skip it when:** You have almost nothing in other people's hands, or you would use it to nag people without a clear ask.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Create a list with three columns: what, who, date asked.
2. Scan your sent mail and chats for requests you made in the last two weeks that are still open and add them.
3. Add a "follow up on" date for each (a few days out for small asks, longer for big ones).
4. Choose a fixed time each week to read it.
**7-day trial:** Add an entry each time you hand something off or ask for something. Track: entries added, and follow-ups you sent on the due date.
**Pairs well with:** Getting Things Done; Inbox zero
**Comes from:** Part of Getting Things Done (David Allen).
**Evidence:** Practice only. It is a tracking convention with no direct testing.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Either
