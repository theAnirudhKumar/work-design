# Methods: managing a team

These methods are for people who lead others: managers, team leads, founders, project leads, and anyone running a group's work, including a parent running a household. They are practices for organising a team's work and your relationship with each person on it, not HR or legal advice; for pay, performance processes, or disputes, follow your organisation's policies and ask the right specialist. Most have little direct research behind them, and the evidence labels say so. Introduce one at a time, tell the team what you are trying and why, and judge it after a week against something you can observe.

## One-on-ones
**One line:** A regular private meeting between a lead and one person, where the agenda belongs mainly to that person.
**Best for:** Leads who only talk to their people in group settings or when something is wrong, so problems and ideas surface late.
**Skip it when:** It becomes a status report to the manager or gets cancelled whenever work is busy; then it is a recurring cost that teaches people their concerns come second.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Choose one direct report and propose 30 minutes every one or two weeks, same slot each time.
2. Tell them the meeting is theirs: they bring topics, you mostly listen and ask.
3. Create a shared note with three headings: their topics, my topics, follow-ups.
4. Ask three starter questions: what is getting in your way, what do you want more or less of from me, what are you thinking about for the next few months.
5. The meeting invite and agenda for a 1:1 can be designed with the separate meeting-design skill.
**7-day trial:** Hold one 1:1 this week. Track: how much of the time the other person spoke, and how many follow-ups you wrote down and later did.
**Pairs well with:** Radical candor; Situational leadership; Skip-level meetings
**Comes from:** Associated with Andy Grove's book High Output Management (1983), which describes the one-on-one as the subordinate's meeting; the practice itself is widespread and its origin is unclear.
**Evidence:** Practice only. Near-universal advice among managers, with little direct testing of its effect.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Team

## Weekly team planning
**One line:** A short fixed meeting at the start of the week where the team agrees its few priorities and who owns each.
**Best for:** Teams that start each week reacting to whatever arrives first, with people unsure what matters most.
**Skip it when:** The week's work is fully set by a queue or shift rota, or the meeting turns into every person reading out a task list.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Book 30 minutes at the start of the week, and ask everyone to bring their top two items.
2. Look back for five minutes: what finished, what slipped, and why.
3. Pick the team's three outcomes for the week, written as results, not activities.
4. Name one owner per outcome and note any dependency between people.
5. Post the list where the team already works, and end on time.
**7-day trial:** Run it once this week and check on Friday. Track: how many of the three outcomes finished, and how many unplanned items took over.
**Pairs well with:** Directly responsible individual; Big rocks; Weekly review
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin.
**Evidence:** Practice only. Widely used in teams, with little direct testing of its effect on results.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Team

## Team retrospective
**One line:** A regular meeting where the team looks back on how it worked, picks one or two changes, and tries them.
**Best for:** Teams that repeat the same frustrations, or finish projects without learning anything from them.
**Skip it when:** Nothing changes afterwards. A retro with no follow-through teaches people that speaking up is pointless. Also hold off when trust is so low that people will not speak honestly.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Book 45 to 60 minutes at the end of a project or sprint, and name a facilitator who is not the loudest voice.
2. Give everyone five quiet minutes to write notes on what went well, what did not, and what to try.
3. Group the notes and let the team vote on the one or two issues that matter most.
4. For each, agree one concrete change, one owner, and a date.
5. Open the next retro by checking those changes first.
**7-day trial:** Run one retro this week. Track: how many people spoke, and whether each agreed change has an owner and a date. Check a week later whether they happened.
**Pairs well with:** Start-stop-continue; Working agreements; Decision log
**Comes from:** Associated with the agile software community; Esther Derby and Diana Larsen's book Agile Retrospectives (2006) is a well-known source, and it sets out five phases: set the stage, gather data, generate insights, decide what to do, close the retrospective.
**Evidence:** Practice only. Widely used, with little direct testing; the idea of learning from experience is sound, but the format is not proven better than alternatives.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Team

## WIP limits
**One line:** Cap how many items can be in progress at once, so people finish work before starting more.
**Best for:** Teams with many things started and few finished, where everything is "nearly done" and requests wait a long time.
**Skip it when:** Work arrives in bursts you cannot control and the cap just creates a queue of angry requesters, or people game it by calling things "waiting" to free a slot.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Count how many items the team has in progress right now.
2. Set a limit below that, such as one or two items per person, or a fixed number per stage.
3. Write the limit at the top of the "doing" column or list.
4. Agree the rule: when the limit is reached, help finish something before pulling anything new.
5. Agree what to do with urgent items, such as one named expedite slot.
**7-day trial:** Hold the limit for the week. Track: how many items finished versus started, and each time the limit blocked someone and what they did instead.
**Pairs well with:** Team Kanban board; Daily standup; Directly responsible individual
**Comes from:** Lean and Kanban practice, with roots in manufacturing flow.
**Evidence:** Practice only. It rests on queueing logic (more work in progress means longer waits) more than on studies of knowledge teams.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Team

## Team Kanban board
**One line:** A shared board of columns showing every piece of team work and where it stands, from waiting to done.
**Best for:** Teams where nobody can see what everyone else is doing, so work is duplicated, dropped, or stuck unnoticed.
**Skip it when:** Nobody updates it. A stale board is worse than none because people trust it. It also adds little for a team of two with a handful of tasks.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Create a board on a wall, whiteboard, or any tool the team already uses.
2. Make columns that match how work really flows, such as Waiting, Doing, Review, Done.
3. Put every current item on a card with one owner's name.
4. Agree who moves cards and when, for example as work changes hands and at the start of the day.
5. Add a WIP limit to the Doing column once the board is in use.
**7-day trial:** Use the board as the only list for a week. Track: how many cards were out of date when you looked, and how many times someone asked for status that the board already answered.
**Pairs well with:** WIP limits; Daily standup; Directly responsible individual
**Comes from:** Kanban for knowledge work is associated with David Anderson, whose book Kanban (2010) popularised it in technology work; Jim Benson and Tonianne DeMaria Barry adapted it for individuals in Personal Kanban (2011). The card idea comes from Toyota's production practice.
**Evidence:** Practice only. Widely used, with little direct testing outside case reports.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Team

## Situational leadership
**One line:** Match how directive or supportive you are to the person's skill and confidence on this particular task, not to the person in general.
**Best for:** Leads who give everyone the same style, hovering over experienced people or leaving newcomers without enough guidance.
**Skip it when:** You use it as a label ("she is a level 2") instead of a conversation; or when you treat the model as proven, since it is popular but weakly tested.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Pick one person and one task you are about to hand over.
2. Rate their skill on that task (new, learning, capable, expert) and their confidence (low or high).
3. Choose your style: tell them how (new), coach and explain (learning), support and ask questions (capable), or step back (expert).
4. Say what you will do out loud, and ask whether it fits what they need.
**7-day trial:** Adjust your style for two tasks this week. Track: how many times you were more or less hands-on than you first would have been, and what the person said when you asked if the support was right.
**Pairs well with:** Delegation levels; One-on-ones; Radical candor
**Comes from:** Paul Hersey and Ken Blanchard, who introduced it around 1969 as the Life Cycle Theory of Leadership; the two later developed separate versions (Hersey's Situational Leadership and Blanchard's SLII).
**Evidence:** Practice only. Popular in management training, but the model itself has been tested weakly; treat it as a prompt for thought, not a rule.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Team

## Radical candor
**One line:** Give feedback that is both direct and caring, so people hear it and act on it.
**Best for:** Leads who either soften feedback until it disappears or deliver it bluntly and lose trust.
**Skip it when:** You have not built any relationship with the person, or the "candor" part is a licence to be harsh. Candor without care is what Scott calls obnoxious aggression. Also use caution where culture or power differences make bluntness land very differently.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Think of one piece of feedback you have been avoiding, about a specific action and not about the person.
2. Write what happened, its effect, and what you would like to see instead, in three short sentences.
3. Add one honest line that shows you care about their success.
4. Choose a private moment soon, and ask first: "Can I give you some feedback on the report?"
5. Listen to their side before you finish.
**7-day trial:** Give one piece of direct, caring feedback and ask for one piece back. Track: how the conversation went and whether the behaviour changed in the following days.
**Pairs well with:** One-on-ones; Situational leadership; Skip-level meetings
**Comes from:** Kim Scott's book Radical Candor (2017), which frames it as caring personally while challenging directly.
**Evidence:** Practice only. Widely read and plausible, but the framework itself has had little direct testing.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Team

## Skip-level meetings
**One line:** Occasional meetings between a leader and people who report to their direct reports, to hear how things look further down.
**Best for:** Leaders of teams with layers, who get only filtered news and want to know what is really getting in people's way.
**Skip it when:** It undermines the manager in between, or the leader acts on what they hear without telling the manager; also when no one will be candid because they fear consequences.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Tell your direct reports first what you plan to do and why: to learn, not to check up on them.
2. Pick two or three people at the next level down, and book 20 to 30 minutes each, or a small group.
3. Prepare three open questions: what is slowing you down, what should we stop doing, what would you change if you were me.
4. Take notes, thank them, and decide what you will share and what stays private.
**7-day trial:** Hold one skip-level conversation this week. Track: one thing you learned that you did not know, and whether you reported back to the manager in the middle.
**Pairs well with:** One-on-ones; Radical candor; Working agreements
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin.
**Evidence:** Practice only. Common management advice, with little direct testing.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Team

## Written weekly updates
**One line:** Each person or team sends a short written update once a week, covering progress, plans, and problems, and the lead reads them all.
**Best for:** Leads who spend meetings gathering status, or teams where people do not know what the others are doing.
**Skip it when:** Updates are written for show or never read; or the team is so small that talking is quicker. A pile of unread updates is paperwork.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Choose a day and time, such as Friday at noon, and one place where updates go.
2. Write a template with four lines: done this week, next week, blockers or help needed, one thing I learned.
3. Ask for no more than 10 lines.
4. Send your own first as an example.
5. Reply to at least one item in each update within a day, so people see it is read.
**7-day trial:** Collect one round of updates this week. Track: how many people sent one by the deadline, and how many blockers you spotted that you would otherwise have found later.
**Pairs well with:** Async-first; Weekly team planning; Decision log
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin; common in remote teams.
**Evidence:** Practice only. Widely used, with little direct testing.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Team

## Team pre-mortem
**One line:** Before a project starts, the team imagines it has failed and writes down why, then acts on the likeliest causes.
**Best for:** Projects with a lot at stake where optimism or politeness keeps risks from being said aloud.
**Skip it when:** The project is small or easily reversed, or the list of worries is never turned into actions; then it is just anxiety on paper.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Gather the team for 30 to 45 minutes and state the project and its goal.
2. Say: "It is six months from now and this failed. Write down every reason why", and give five minutes of silent writing.
3. Collect the reasons, group similar ones, and let the quietest people read theirs out first.
4. Pick the top three and, for each, agree one thing to do now and who owns it.
5. The what-could-go-wrong skill can run this with you.
**7-day trial:** Run one pre-mortem on a real upcoming project this week. Track: how many risks were new to you, and whether each top risk got an owner and a first action within the week.
**Pairs well with:** Working backwards; Decision log; Directly responsible individual
**Comes from:** Associated with psychologist Gary Klein, who described the technique in "Performing a Project Premortem" (Harvard Business Review, September 2007).
**Evidence:** Practice only. Plausible and popular, and it builds on lab work on imagining an event as already having happened, but the team premortem has had little direct testing.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Team
