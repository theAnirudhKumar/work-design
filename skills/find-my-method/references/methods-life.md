# Methods: personal life and admin

These methods are practical organising for the unglamorous side of life: errands, meals, paperwork, shared household planning, and digital clutter. They are not financial, medical, or legal advice, and nothing here replaces a qualified professional for money, health, or legal questions. Most are folk practice with little direct testing, and the cards say so. Pick one that removes a recurring annoyance, run it for a week, and keep it only if the annoyance shrinks.

## Life admin hour
**One line:** One fixed weekly hour for forms, bills, appointments, and other small chores, so they stop leaking into the rest of the week.
**Best for:** A pile of small overdue tasks (booking the dentist, renewing, replying, returning) that nag but never feel urgent.
**Skip it when:** You have almost no admin, or you would use the hour to avoid larger, more important work.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Choose a weekly slot and put it in your calendar now.
2. Open a single list titled "Admin" and dump everything you can think of for five minutes.
3. Mark the three that take under 10 minutes each.
4. Do one of them right now.
**7-day trial:** Hold the hour once this week, working top to bottom on the list. Track: number of items finished, and how many new ones appeared.
**Pairs well with:** Errand batching; Budget check-in; Digital declutter
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin.
**Evidence:** Practice only. Widely recommended, with little direct testing.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Either

## Errand batching
**One line:** Group errands by location and route into one or two trips instead of many small ones.
**Best for:** Frequent small trips that eat evenings and weekends, especially if you rely on a car or have limited free time.
**Skip it when:** Items are perishable or urgent, or batching creates a long outing you keep postponing.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Write every errand you need to do this week.
2. Next to each, note where it is and any opening hours.
3. Group by area, and by what is open when.
4. Choose a day and order for each group, and add them to your calendar.
**7-day trial:** Do all errands in at most two trips this week. Track: number of trips, and time or cost saved compared with a normal week, as you estimate it.
**Pairs well with:** Life admin hour; Meal prep batching; One in one out
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin.
**Evidence:** Practice only. Common sense that is widely used, with little direct testing.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Either

## Meal prep batching
**One line:** Cook or prepare parts of several meals in one session so weeknights need only assembly or reheating.
**Best for:** Busy weeks, shift work, and households where "what's for dinner?" is a daily stressor.
**Skip it when:** You dislike leftovers, have little fridge or freezer space, or follow dietary guidance from a professional that this might not suit; check with them if so.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Pick three dinners for the coming week and write the ingredients.
2. Choose a prep slot (an hour on a weekend or a quiet evening).
3. List what can be done ahead: wash and chop, cook grains, make a sauce.
4. Add missing items to your shopping list and note storage containers needed.
**7-day trial:** Prep once, then eat from it for the week. Track: how many weeknight dinners needed under 15 minutes, and how much food went uneaten. This is a general routine, not nutrition or food safety advice; follow proper storage guidance.
**Pairs well with:** Errand batching; Family planning meeting; Weekly time together
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin.
**Evidence:** Practice only. Widely used, with little direct testing of its effects.
**Effort to set up:** Medium.   **Works for:** Either

## Family planning meeting
**One line:** A short regular meeting where a household looks at the week ahead, shares the load, and settles clashes before they happen.
**Best for:** Households with several schedules, shared chores, school or care logistics, or repeated "I didn't know" arguments.
**Skip it when:** It becomes a lecture or complaints session, or the household is small enough that a quick chat covers it.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Pick a 15-minute slot at the same time each week and invite everyone old enough.
2. Look at the shared calendar for the next seven days.
3. Ask each person: anything I need help with, anything coming up?
4. Assign who handles what (lifts, meals, chores) and write it where all can see.
5. End with something pleasant, such as choosing a weekend plan.
**7-day trial:** Hold it once this week. Track: number of clashes or surprises that week, and whether each person felt heard (ask one question at the end).
**Pairs well with:** Weekly time together; Meal prep batching; Life admin hour
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin.
**Evidence:** Practice only. Used in many households, with little direct testing.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Either

## Digital declutter
**One line:** Spend a short session clearing what clutters your devices, then set one rule to keep it from refilling.
**Best for:** Overloaded inboxes, photo libraries, desktop files, or notifications that make finding and focusing hard.
**Skip it when:** You would use it to avoid real work, or you risk deleting something you need to keep (tax, legal, medical, or work records); keep those and check retention rules first.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Pick one target: inbox, downloads folder, phone home screen, or notifications.
2. Set a 10-minute timer.
3. Delete or archive the obvious junk; do not sort anything carefully.
4. Turn off notifications for apps you do not need to act on.
5. Write one keeping rule, such as "downloads is emptied every Friday".
**7-day trial:** Declutter one target a day for 10 minutes. Track: items cleared, and how often you could not find something versus before, by your own count.
**Pairs well with:** Life admin hour; One in one out; Budget check-in
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin.
**Evidence:** Practice only. Widely recommended, with little direct testing.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Solo

## One in one out
**One line:** When something new comes in, something similar goes out, so a category never grows.
**Best for:** Overflowing wardrobes, cupboards, bookshelves, kitchen gadgets, or toy boxes.
**Skip it when:** You already have too much in the category; start with a one-off clear-out first. It also does not suit things you truly need to stock up on.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Pick one category (shoes, mugs, books).
2. Set a limit: the space it fits in, or a number.
3. Put a box or bag near the space labelled "out".
4. Next time you bring something in, move one thing to the box.
**7-day trial:** Apply the rule to one category for the week. Track: items in versus items out, and whether you found it easy or annoying to choose.
**Pairs well with:** Digital declutter; Errand batching; Life admin hour
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin.
**Evidence:** Practice only. A common household rule, with little direct testing.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Either

## Budget check-in
**One line:** A short weekly habit of looking at your own money-in and money-out numbers so nothing surprises you. This is a habit of looking, not financial advice.
**Best for:** Not knowing where money goes, or avoiding opening bank apps; it builds familiarity, not a plan.
**Skip it when:** Looking at money triggers real distress; consider a trusted person or a qualified adviser to help. It cannot tell you what to do with your money.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Choose a weekly time and add it to your calendar.
2. Open your bank or card statement for the last seven days.
3. Write three numbers: money in, money out, and the largest single outgoing.
4. Note any charge you do not recognise or want to cancel.
**7-day trial:** Do the check-in once, with a short note on what surprised you. Track: whether you did it, and the three numbers; compare them next week.
**Pairs well with:** Life admin hour; Digital declutter; Weekly time together
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin.
**Evidence:** Practice only. Looking at your own spending is widely recommended, with little direct testing of this routine.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Either

## Weekly time together
**One line:** A protected slot each week for a partner, family, or friends, put in the calendar like any other commitment.
**Best for:** Relationships that get the leftovers of the week, with time together always giving way to work and chores.
**Skip it when:** It becomes an obligation that adds stress, or the other person has not agreed; a forced slot can backfire, so make it theirs too.
**Start in 10 minutes:**
1. Ask the other person what kind of time they would enjoy (walk, meal, game, nothing planned).
2. Pick a recurring slot that works for both.
3. Put it in both calendars with a title that is not "maybe".
4. Agree on phones away for that hour.
**7-day trial:** Hold one slot this week. Track: whether it happened, and one word each for how it felt.
**Pairs well with:** Family planning meeting; Meal prep batching; Errand batching
**Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin.
**Evidence:** Practice only. Widely recommended, with little direct testing of this routine.
**Effort to set up:** Low.   **Works for:** Either
