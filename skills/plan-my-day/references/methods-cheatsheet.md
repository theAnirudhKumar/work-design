# Methods cheat-sheet for plan-my-day

Short notes on the methods this skill applies, so it works when installed alone. Evidence labels follow one rubric: Strong (repeated controlled studies support the core mechanism), Some (small, indirect or related-setting support), Mixed (studies conflict or depend on the person), Practice only (widely used, little direct testing). When unsure, the label is Practice only. No figures are claimed.

## Most-important tasks (MIT)
Pick one to three things that make the day a success; do them first.
- **Use it to**: choose the must-dos in Step 4.
- **Backfires when**: the person picks three huge items, or picks the same urgent-but-minor thing every day, or the list quietly grows to five.
- **Evidence**: Practice only. Choosing few priorities is common advice; the specific count of three is a convention.

## Time blocking
Give each task a slot on the calendar instead of a place on a list.
- **Use it to**: place the must-dos and batches in Step 6.
- **Backfires when**: the day is unpredictable and blocks are treated as promises, so every overrun feels like failure. Fix: buffers, slack and a slip rule. Also backfires with no estimates; a block is only as good as its guess.
- **Evidence**: Practice only. It makes the day's limits visible, which is plausibly the useful part; direct testing is thin.

## Buffers and slack
Empty time after meetings and unscheduled time in the day to absorb overrun.
- **Use it to**: Step 3 and Step 6. Rule of thumb: plan about two thirds of what is left. It is a starting point, not a measured value; adjust after a week of seeing how much the person actually completes.
- **Backfires when**: the slack is filled with new requests, or the person feels guilty about unscheduled time. Name it "slack" so it is visible and not borrowed.
- **Evidence**: Strong that people underestimate how long tasks take (the planning fallacy is well documented). Practice only for the specific slack proportion.

## Ivy Lee method
Each evening, write the six most important tasks for tomorrow in rank order. Next day work them one at a time in that order; carry unfinished ones over.
- **Use it to**: shape a day for someone who loses time deciding what is next.
- **Backfires when**: the day is driven by other people's requests (support, caregiving, on-call), where an ordered list cannot hold. Also when the person treats six items as a quota.
- **Evidence**: Practice only. A popular story attributes it to a consultant named Ivy Lee advising a steel executive; the details are anecdotal.

## 1-3-5
One big task, three medium, five small per day.
- **Use it to**: give a mixed day a shape with a ceiling.
- **Backfires when**: nine items do not fit the real capacity, which is common. Then drop to 1-2-3 and say so. Also awkward when the day has one enormous task and nothing else.
- **Evidence**: Practice only. Folk practice, no single origin.

## Day theming
Give a day a theme (writing, calls, admin) and keep other work off it.
- **Use it to**: decide what enters the plan when the person already has a theme for today.
- **Backfires when**: the job cannot wait for its theme day (patients, customers, children), or the theme day gets hit by an emergency and the week's theme collapses. Works best for flexible roles.
- **Evidence**: Practice only.

## Pomodoro and timed rounds
Work in short fixed rounds (commonly 25 minutes) with short breaks, counting rounds.
- **Use it to**: shape a block for someone who drifts or starts slowly. Claude cannot keep time, so the person sets the timer.
- **Backfires when**: the work needs longer unbroken stretches, or the timer interrupts a good run and the person stops. Then lengthen the round or ignore the bell while going well.
- **Evidence**: Some. Studies on short breaks support that they help; the particular 25 and 5 split is a convention. Not medical advice.

## Task batching
Do similar small tasks in one window (messages, forms, calls).
- **Use it to**: the shallow windows in Step 6.
- **Backfires when**: a batch delays something urgent or a person is waiting on a reply, or when "batch" becomes an hour in the inbox. Cap the window.
- **Evidence**: Some. Lab studies show that switching between tasks has a cost; how much batching helps in real jobs is less tested.

## Eat the frog
Do the hardest or most dreaded task first.
- **Use it to**: place the must-do at the start of the peak window when the dread item is also the important one.
- **Backfires when**: the hardest task is not the most important, or the person is not sharp early. Then it burns the best hour on the wrong thing.
- **Evidence**: Practice only. Often attributed to a writer who attributed it to Mark Twain; the attribution is disputed.

## Minimum viable day
Three small wins, finishable on a bad day, plus the commitments that cannot move.
- **Use it to**: the bad-day mode.
- **Backfires when**: used every day as an excuse to skip the real plan. If the bad days are most days, hand off to `protect-my-focus` or `balance-my-life` by name.
- **Evidence**: Practice only. Small, finishable wins are a common coaching tactic; direct testing is limited.
