# Methods cheat-sheet for plan-my-week

Short notes on the methods this skill applies, so it works when installed alone. Evidence labels follow one rubric: Strong (repeated controlled studies support the core mechanism), Some (small, indirect or related-setting support), Mixed (studies conflict or depend on the person), Practice only (widely used, little direct testing). When unsure, the label is Practice only. No figures are claimed.

## Weekly review
A fixed, short session to look back, clear loose ends and decide the week ahead. Popularised in GTD-style systems.
- **Use it to**: Step 1. Wins, slips, carry-overs.
- **Backfires when**: it grows into an hour of tidying every list and replaces the actual work, or when it is skipped after one missed week and never restarted. Keep it to a short look back and a forward plan; if a week is missed, restart without catching up.
- **Evidence**: Practice only. Reflecting on progress is plausibly useful; the specific weekly format is untested as such.

## Outcomes over tasks (weekly rule of three)
Choose two or three results for the week; everything else is secondary.
- **Use it to**: Step 2.
- **Backfires when**: outcomes are vague ("make progress"), or three large outcomes exceed the hours. Check capacity before accepting them.
- **Evidence**: Practice only. Folk practice, no single origin.

## Ideal week
Sketch the week as repeating blocks (focus, meetings, admin, personal) before the week fills up, then defend the shape.
- **Use it to**: Step 3.
- **Backfires when**: it is built as a fantasy that ignores fixed commitments, or treated as binding when a job is driven by others' requests. It is a default shape to return to, not a rule.
- **Evidence**: Practice only.

## Protected focus blocks
Named, recurring blocks of uninterrupted time on the calendar for the hard work.
- **Use it to**: Step 3. Blocks are linked to an outcome, placed early in the week, and defended with notice to others.
- **Backfires when**: the person has no control over their calendar, or the blocks are placed in low-energy hours. Then fewer, shorter blocks in the best available hours. See `protect-my-focus` for negotiating them.
- **Evidence**: Some. Research on interruptions shows they cost time and quality of work; how much a calendar block prevents is less tested. Related idea: long stretches suit makers and short ones suit managers, as one widely read essay framed it. That framing is an argument, not a result.

## Theme days
Assign a type of work to a day.
- **Use it to**: Step 4, only for roles with enough control.
- **Backfires when**: the work arrives from others on its own schedule (clinical work, support, teaching, young children). Then use half-days or none.
- **Evidence**: Practice only.

## Limit work in progress
Start fewer things so more finish. In this skill: three outcomes, and every carry-over gets a verdict.
- **Use it to**: Steps 1 and 2, the carry/delegate/drop rule.
- **Backfires when**: it blocks urgent new work that really must start, or it is applied as a hard cap where the job is reactive. Leave room for a stated exception.
- **Evidence**: Some. The idea is well grounded in production and queueing settings; applying it to one person's week is mostly practice.

## If-then planning (implementation intentions)
Decide the cue and the action in advance: "If X happens, then I do Y."
- **Use it to**: Step 5, the one risk.
- **Backfires when**: the cue is vague or never occurs ("if I have time"). Choose a cue the person will actually meet.
- **Evidence**: Strong that specifying when and where to act helps people follow through on goals, across many studies; effects vary by person and goal.

## Slack
Plan about two thirds of available hours; leave the rest empty by design.
- **Use it to**: Steps 2 and 3. A rule of thumb, not a measured fact. Raise it for reactive jobs, lower it for a quiet week.
- **Backfires when**: it is quietly filled with new commitments. Name the blocks "slack".
- **Evidence**: Strong that people underestimate task time. Practice only for the proportion.

## One personal item
A single non-work block placed on the calendar like any other.
- **Use it to**: Step 6.
- **Backfires when**: it is a goal in disguise ("get fit") or the first thing cut when work runs over. Make it small and specific, and put it on the calendar.
- **Evidence**: Practice only. Rest and time away from work are generally regarded as helpful for wellbeing; this is general wellbeing practice, not medical advice.
