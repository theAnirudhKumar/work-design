# Focus formats: a short cheat-sheet

Evidence labels: Strong, Some, Mixed, Practice only. Be honest with the user: none of these formats has been shown to beat the others for everyone. They are structures for starting and stopping, and the useful test is the user's own results over several blocks.

## How to choose

| Task and state | Start with |
| :--- | :--- |
| Shallow or admin work, many small items, low energy | Pomodoro 25/5 |
| Analytical or study work, steady energy, 1 to 2 hours free | 52/17 or a plain hour |
| Deep work needing a long ramp-up (writing, design, coding a hard problem) | 90-minute block |
| Open-ended creative work, or work where stopping on a timer feels wrong | Flowtime (or 90 minutes) |
| Hard to start, flat energy, dread | 10 to 15 minute block, Pomodoro-style, permission to stop |
| Interruptible job (care, support, parenting) | Short blocks, 15 to 25 minutes, with a note to resume |
| Just need the hour protected, no ceremony | Plain one-hour block |

## Pomodoro (25 minutes work, 5 minutes break)

- **What:** Work for 25 minutes on one task, take 5 minutes off, repeat. After about four rounds, take a longer break (15 to 30 minutes).
- **Comes from:** Francesco Cirillo.
- **Good for:** starting, admin, revision, anything that benefits from a visible end and frequent resets.
- **Backfires when:** the work needs a long ramp-up, so the timer rings just as the thinking gets going; or when the user ends up checking the clock instead of working.
- **Evidence:** Practice only. Widely used; little direct testing of the specific 25/5 split. The general ideas (breaks help sustain effort, a defined end helps starting) have some support, but not for this exact ratio.

## 52/17

- **What:** 52 minutes of work, 17 minutes away from the screen.
- **Comes from:** popularised from a workplace time-tracking company's analysis of its own users; it is an observation, not a controlled trial, and the exact numbers should not be treated as special.
- **Good for:** steady analytical work when 25 minutes feels too choppy.
- **Backfires when:** 17 minutes of break turns into 40, or the break is used for email and chat.
- **Evidence:** Practice only.

## 90-minute block

- **What:** One block of about 90 minutes on one task, then a real break of 15 to 30 minutes, with at most two or three blocks a day.
- **Idea behind it:** a claimed natural rhythm of focus and fatigue across the day.
- **Good for:** deep work with a long ramp-up; creative and complex tasks.
- **Backfires when:** the user cannot sustain it yet (start at 45 to 60 and extend), the environment is interruptible, or the block ends up being 90 minutes of half-attention. A ninety-minute block with a phone in reach is not a block.
- **Evidence:** Mixed. The underlying rhythm claim is contested; the practical use as "a long, protected block" is common sense with some support.

## Flowtime

- **What:** Start working, note the start time, stop when focus fades naturally, note the end time, then take a break proportionate to the work (a short break after a short stretch, a longer one after a long stretch). Track the stretch lengths to learn your own pattern.
- **Comes from:** Folk practice, no single origin to rely on.
- **Good for:** creative and open-ended work; people who find fixed timers disruptive.
- **Backfires when:** there is no end condition, so the "natural stop" is a drift to the phone, or when the user needs a hard stop to start at all.
- **Evidence:** Practice only.

## A plain one-hour block

- **What:** An hour on one task, a clear done-line, phone away, no further structure.
- **Good for:** people who dislike timers and rules; a first step before adopting anything else.
- **Backfires when:** there is no done-line or no timer, so the hour blurs.
- **Evidence:** Practice only. The mechanism (single task, defined end, fewer interruptions) is the part that carries weight.

## What they all share

The parts that do the work are the same across formats: one task, a defined end, the phone out of reach, stray thoughts parked rather than acted on, and a real break. If a format is not working, adjust those first.

## Notes on parts of the card

- **Parking lot:** a written place to put stray tasks and thoughts so they do not have to be held or acted on. Practical, low-cost, and widely used; treat as practice-only evidence.
- **Body doubling:** working alongside another person, physically or on a call. Popular among people who struggle to start; evidence is mostly anecdotal and early. Offer it as something to try. It backfires if the other person chats or if arranging it becomes the task.
- **Shrinking the first step:** a common technique for starting. Backfires if the "tiny step" is left as the whole session without a follow-on.
- **Phone away:** reducing nearby temptation is a plain idea and the effect of mere phone presence on attention has been studied with mixed findings; the practical advice is cheap, so keep it. Do not insist on it where the user must be reachable.
- **Breaks:** general wellbeing practice, not medical advice. For eye strain, back or wrist pain, or any health condition, follow a professional's advice.
